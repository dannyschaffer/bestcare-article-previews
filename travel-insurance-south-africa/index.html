Travel insurance: what South Africans underestimate before a December trip

Most South Africans think about travel insurance for a few minutes at checkout, if at all. The gaps show up later: a hospital abroad that wants proof of payment first, a card benefit with a lower limit than expected, or a cancellation the policy doesn’t recognise. Here’s what travel insurance in South Africa covers, who won’t pay if you don’t have it, and what to check before December.

Quick answer Travel insurance pays for emergency medical treatment, evacuation and repatriation abroad, and usually for cancellation, luggage and liability too. Without it, those costs are yours: DIRCO’s consular services are “of an advisory and non-financial nature”, and it will not “pay hotel, legal, medical or any other bills” (DIRCO, Consular Services). DIRCO also warns that “medical treatment can be withheld by the foreign country if a person has no proof of funds or travel insurance” (DIRCO, Advice for South Africans travelling abroad). Check the medical limit, pre-existing conditions and cancellation reasons before you rely on any policy, including free card cover.

What DIRCO will and won’t pay for abroad

If something goes wrong overseas, the South African government can help you make contact and arrangements, but it won’t pay. DIRCO says it is “strongly recommended that you take out travel insurance before travelling abroad”, and that it “should cover hospitalisation and related medical costs as well as a possible emergency evacuation” (DIRCO).

Its list of services it won’t provide is blunt. Consular officers will not pay medical or hotel bills, will not pay travelling expenses, and will not “pay for cremations, burials or the repatriation of mortal remains to South Africa” (DIRCO, Consular Services). Its guidelines repeat that hospital bills, flights, accommodation, repatriation and legal fees are all “for the account of the client” (DIRCO Guidelines on Consular Services (2023)).

So the real question isn’t whether you need cover, but what kind and how much. That starts with what a travel policy actually includes.

What travel insurance covers

A travel policy is several covers in one, and the medical part is the one that matters most. DIRCO’s own checklist is “hospital treatment, medical evacuation and even death” (DIRCO). The rest protects the money you spent on the trip and your belongings.

Card cover and medical aid: check the limits

Free travel insurance from a credit card, and emergency cover from a medical aid, are useful, but neither is automatically enough. Both are benefits with their own rules, and the rules are set out in documents most people never read.

Card-linked cover is offered as one of a card programme’s benefits, alongside things like lounge access and roadside assistance (Moneyweb (2023)). Before relying on it, ask for the benefit terms and check:

  • the medical limit, and whether it is enough for where you’re going;
  • whether the whole ticket had to be paid with the card, and whether you need to activate the cover;
  • age limits, maximum trip length and whether your spouse and children are included;
  • whether pre-existing conditions, cancellation and luggage are covered at all.

Medical aid travel benefits vary even more. Scheme rules published by the Council for Medical Schemes in 2026 show options with emergency cover of R5 million “per 90-day journey” and other options with “No benefit” for international cover at all (Council for Medical Schemes (2026)). Emergency-only cover usually won’t help with cancellation or lost luggage, and a long trip can outlast the journey limit.

The FSCA’s advice applies to every option: make sure you “understand all the terms and conditions (including the exclusions and limitations) in your contract and how they respond to your situation” (FSCA consumer tips (2021)). If the limits fall short, you can buy a separate policy or top-up cover. For some trips, the minimum is set by law.

What travel insurance do I need for a Schengen visa?

For a Schengen visa you need travel medical insurance of at least EUR 30 000, valid in every Schengen country, for the whole trip. Article 15 of the EU Visa Code requires cover for “repatriation for medical reasons, urgent medical attention and/or emergency hospital treatment or death”, and says the insurance “shall be valid throughout the territory of the Member States and cover the entire period of the person’s intended stay or transit. The minimum coverage shall be EUR 30 000” (Regulation (EC) No 810/2009, art. 15).

For a multiple-entry visa, you need cover for the first trip and must sign that you understand you need it for later trips too. The embassy or visa centre will want a certificate showing these points, so check that your policy, including any card cover, can produce one. Other countries set their own rules, which change, so check the official requirements for your destination when you book. Whatever the destination, the other big question is your health before you leave.

Pre-existing conditions and disclosure

Pre-existing medical conditions are where travel claims most often go wrong. DIRCO notes that “pre-existing conditions and the risk associated with your destination or the activities you plan to partake in can influence the travel insurance cover you need” (DIRCO). Policies handle them in different ways: some exclude them, some cover them once declared, and some cover them at a lower limit.

The rule that protects you is simple: declare every condition, medication and recent treatment when you buy, even if it seems minor or well controlled. Non-disclosure is a long-standing reason for declined claims across all types of insurance, and the National Financial Ombud publishes a whole category of decided cases on it (NFO, non-disclosure cases). Older travellers, pregnant travellers and anyone planning adventure activities should read the relevant exclusions with particular care. The same care applies to the reasons a trip can be cancelled.

Trip cancellation: where claims get stuck

Cancellation cover pays only for the reasons the policy lists, and only for events after the cover started. A doctor’s note on its own isn’t always enough. The National Financial Ombud explains that “many travel policies do not provide unrestricted cover for every medically advised cancellation. Instead, cover may be limited to specific insured medical events”, and that disputes are usually “not about whether the doctor’s advice was genuine, but whether the medical condition falls within the scope of cover promised by the policy” (NFO in FAnews, August 2026).

Timing matters too. In one reported case, a couple couldn’t claim on their travel policy because it “was not purchased within 48 hours of buying the airplane tickets” (Moneyweb (2020)). If something goes wrong on the trip

Call the policy’s emergency assistance number before you go to a private hospital, if you can, because many policies require the assistance company to approve or arrange treatment and evacuation. Keep every document: medical reports, receipts, airline property irregularity reports for luggage and police reports for theft.

Claim as soon as you can after you get home, using the same evidence approach as any other claim; our guide on how to make an insurance claim step by step sets out the habits that help. Key takeaways

  • DIRCO strongly recommends travel insurance and won’t pay medical, hotel, travel or repatriation bills abroad.
  • Some countries withhold treatment without proof of funds or insurance, according to DIRCO.
  • A Schengen visa needs at least EUR 30 000 of medical cover, valid in all Schengen states for the whole stay.
  • Free card cover and medical aid travel benefits have limits on cover, trip length and who is included; read them before you rely on them.
  • Declare every pre-existing condition; non-disclosure is a common reason claims fail.
  • Cancellation cover pays only for listed reasons, so buy cover when you book and read that section first.

Travel insurance FAQs

General answers about travel insurance for South Africans.

What does travel insurance cover?

Travel insurance commonly covers emergency medical and hospital costs abroad, medical evacuation and repatriation, the costs of returning mortal remains, trip cancellation or cutting a trip short for listed reasons, lost or delayed luggage, and personal liability. Limits, excesses and exclusions vary by policy.

Do I need travel insurance if my credit card gives me free cover?

Card-linked cover can be useful, but it is a benefit with its own conditions. Check the medical limit, whether the whole ticket had to be paid on the card, age and trip length limits, and whether pre-existing conditions and family members are included. If the limits are too low for your destination, you can buy extra cover.

Does my medical aid cover me overseas?

Some medical scheme options include emergency cover abroad, often capped per journey and limited to a set number of days, and some options have no international benefit at all. Check your option’s rules for the limit, the trip length, and whether only emergencies are covered.

What travel insurance do I need for a Schengen visa?

The EU Visa Code requires travel medical insurance of at least EUR 30 000 that covers medical repatriation, urgent medical attention, emergency hospital treatment or death, is valid in all Schengen states and covers the whole stay. Check the specific embassy’s requirements when you apply.

Will DIRCO help if I get sick abroad?

DIRCO’s consular help is advisory and non-financial. It can help with contact and logistics if you are in hospital abroad, but it does not pay medical, hotel, legal or travel bills, or for repatriating mortal remains. Those costs are your own unless you have insurance.

Does travel insurance cover pre-existing medical conditions?

Some policies exclude them, some cover them after you declare them, and some cover them at lower limits. Declare every condition when you buy the policy. If you don’t, a related claim can be declined.

When should I buy travel insurance?

As soon as you book. Cancellation cover only helps for events after the policy starts, and some policies have conditions linked to when you bought your flights. Buying early also gives you time to read the wording before you travel.

Can I cancel my trip on doctor’s advice and claim?

Not always. The National Financial Ombud has explained that many travel policies don’t cover every medically advised cancellation, and that cover may be limited to specific insured medical events. Check which medical reasons your policy lists before you cancel.
