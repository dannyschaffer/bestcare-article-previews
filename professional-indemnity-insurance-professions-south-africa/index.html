Professional indemnity insurance: which South African professions actually need it?

Only a few professions have professional indemnity cover written into their rules. For everyone else, the push comes from professional bodies, clients and tenders, and from the fact that a client can claim years after the work is done. Here’s what each profession’s rules say, checked against the primary text.

Quick answer Professional indemnity (PI) insurance responds when a client claims your advice or service caused them a financial loss. In South Africa it’s built into the rules for practising attorneys (through the Fidelity Fund certificate and the profession’s group policy), authorised financial services providers (minimums of R1 million or R5 million, or a guarantee), and architects doing work for clients. Regulations also require it for psychologists in independent practice. For doctors, engineers, quantity surveyors, accountants, tax practitioners, estate agents and consultants, we found no law that requires it. Their professional bodies, clients and tenders often do.

What professional indemnity insurance is for

PI cover is for claims that your professional work, such as advice, a design, a report or a calculation, caused someone a financial loss. It’s different from public liability, which is about injury to people or damage to their property. A consulting engineer whose design error delays a building project faces a PI-type claim. A visitor who trips over a cable in the same engineer’s office raises a public liability question.

Profession by profession: what the rules say

We checked each profession’s Act, regulations and council codes. Where a rule exists, it’s quoted below. Where we found none, we say so. Rules can change, so treat this as a starting point and check with your regulator.

Two things stand out. First, a “no” in the table doesn’t mean no one will ask: clients, panels and tenders often set their own minimums. The attorneys’ own insurer says some organisations “may insist” on more cover than the group policy before they’ll instruct a firm (LPIIF). Second, even where cover is compulsory, the compulsory amount is a minimum, not a measure of what a serious claim could cost.

Why it matters even where it isn’t compulsory

Every professional can be sued for negligence, and the claim can arrive long after the work is done. The test courts apply hasn’t changed in a century: a professional isn’t expected to show the highest possible skill, but “is bound to employ reasonable skill and care”, judged against “the general level of skill and diligence possessed and exercised at the time by the members of the branch of the profession to which the practitioner belongs” (Van Wyk v Lewis 1924 AD 438).

Timing is the part people underestimate. Under the Prescription Act, most claims prescribe after three years (section 11(d)). But that period only starts when the client knows who caused the loss and the facts behind it, or could have found out with reasonable care (section 12(3)). A flawed design, tax structure or contract can go unnoticed for years. In the attorneys’ group scheme, missed prescription on Road Accident Fund claims “has perennially posted the biggest risk, both in the number and value of claims”, out of 335 claims notified in 2024/25 (LPIIF Integrated Report 2025).

Complaints don’t only go to court. In 2024/25, the FAIS Ombud received 3,382 complaints within its jurisdiction against financial services providers and awarded R31.7 million in compensation (FAIS Ombud).

Claims-made cover, retroactive dates and retirement

Most PI policies are “claims-made”: they respond to claims first made while the policy is in force, not to when the work was done. The attorneys’ group policy, for example, responds where “the Claim is first made against the Insured during the current Insurance Year”. In February 2026, the Western Cape High Court confirmed that under a claims-made PI policy, cover is triggered when a claim is made and reported to the insurer, not when the negligent conduct occurred (Deneys, May 2026). That has three practical consequences:

Check the retroactive date

Work done before your policy’s retroactive date may not be covered. As the same law-firm note puts it, a retroactive date “does not extend the period of insurance”, but lets claims about past work fall under the current policy if you notify properly.

Report circumstances early

In the same case, the court found that failing to tell the insurer about a client’s complaint when it first arose was a material breach on its own. If a client complains or something goes wrong, tell your insurer, even if no claim has been made yet.

Plan for when you stop

If your policy lapses when you retire or close, a later claim about old work may have nowhere to go. Ask about run-off cover. SAIT’s code says continuing cover “must be arranged” after a member stops practising. The attorneys’ group scheme covers retired practitioners for past work, but suggests checking whether top-up cover needs run-off.

Keep in mind: PI policies often exclude some things that look like professional mistakes. The attorneys’ group scheme, for instance, doesn’t cover theft of trust money, which is the Fidelity Fund’s job. Read the exclusions on your own policy, especially for fines, refunds of fees and cyber incidents.

Key takeaways

  • PI cover is written into the rules for practising attorneys, FAIS providers, architects doing client work and psychologists in independent practice.
  • Doctors, engineers, quantity surveyors, accountants, tax practitioners, estate agents and consultants have no legal PI requirement that we found.
  • Professional bodies, clients and tenders often ask for PI, sometimes with their own minimums.
  • A claim can be brought three years from when the client discovers the problem, which may be years after the work.
  • Most PI is claims-made, so the retroactive date, early notification and run-off cover all matter.

Professional indemnity FAQs

General answers about professional indemnity insurance in South Africa. Your regulator’s rules and your policy wording decide what applies to you.

Is professional indemnity insurance compulsory in South Africa?

Not for most professions. It is built into the rules for practising attorneys, authorised financial services providers and architects doing work for clients. For most other professionals, including doctors, engineers, accountants and consultants, no law we found requires it, although professional bodies, clients and tenders often ask for it.

Do doctors need professional indemnity insurance by law?

Regulations that required it for health practitioners in independent practice were published in August 2010 and repealed in October 2010. Separate 2004 regulations require psychologists in independent practice to hold indemnity cover. Hospitals, practices and locum agencies may still ask for proof of indemnity, so check any contract you sign.

How much professional indemnity cover do financial advisers need?

Board Notice 123 of 2009 sets minimums: R1 million for Category I and IV providers, and R1 million or R5 million for Category II providers, depending on whether they hold clients’ funds. A guarantee can be used instead of insurance. Insurers and banks are excluded.

What does claims-made mean on a professional indemnity policy?

The policy responds to claims first made, and reported, while it is in force, not to when the work was done. Work done before the policy started is only included back to the retroactive date on your schedule, and late notification can put cover at risk.

How long after a job can a client claim against me?

Generally three years from when the client knew, or could reasonably have found out, who caused the loss and the facts behind it. That date can be years after the work was finished.

What happens to my professional indemnity cover when I retire?

Because most PI cover is claims-made, a claim about past work made after your policy lapses may not be covered. Ask about run-off cover before you stop practising. SAIT’s code, for example, says continuing cover must be arranged after a member stops practising.

What’s the difference between professional indemnity and public liability?

Professional indemnity responds to claims that your advice or service caused someone a financial loss. Public liability responds to claims that your business caused injury or property damage. Many businesses consider both.
