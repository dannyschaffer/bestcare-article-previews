When the bakkie becomes a business asset: private cover vs commercial vehicle insurance

A bakkie that started as the family vehicle often ends up carrying tools, stock or staff. Here’s how insurers classify vehicle use, why the class on your schedule has to match what you actually do, and what to ask before your next job.

Quick answer Motor insurers usually classify a vehicle’s use as private, business or commercial, and the cover and price depend on that class. Private use generally covers social trips and commuting to your regular workplace. Business use extends to work travel such as visiting clients, and commercial use applies when the vehicle itself earns an income, such as deliveries or carrying goods for reward (Short-term Ombud guidance via Moonstone, 2021). If your bakkie is used for more than its schedule says, a claim may be rejected. Tell your insurer or broker how the vehicle is really used.

Private, business or commercial: how insurers classify use

The use class on your schedule describes what the insurer agreed to cover. It’s one of the details your premium is built on, and a claim is usually tested against it. The Short-term Insurance Ombud set out the three common classes, and the definitions below come from that guidance. Each insurer’s wording sets the exact boundaries.

The distinction runs through insurance law as well. The Insurance Act divides motor insurance into personal lines and commercial lines sub-classes (Insurance Act 18 of 2017, Schedule 2). Our commercial vehicle and fleet insurance page explains how they work.

The line between the classes can be crossed on a single trip.

One business trip on private cover

In a case summarised in the Short-term Ombud’s guidance, a driver with private-use cover had a collision on his way to meet a friend for dinner to discuss a business proposition. The insurer rejected the claim because the car was being used for business at the time, and the ombud upheld the rejection as a proper reading of the policy (Moonstone, February 2021).

The trip was a dinner. What decided the claim was its purpose. That’s why the classes matter for anyone with a side business: quoting a job, collecting materials or dropping off an order can all count as business use.

The same guidance covers lift clubs. If the passengers’ contributions leave you with any profit after petrol, the ombud said you should tell your insurer that the vehicle is being used for commercial purposes. If you carry passengers through an app, our guide to e-hailing insurance covers that use.

Disclosure works both ways. The NFO’s Lead Ombud for non-life insurance has said insurers must “create proper disclosure, so that people are given an opportunity to disclose”, rather than doing that work at claim stage (Moonstone, May 2026). Your part is to answer the questions accurately, and to say so when your use changes.

The tax point is worth a moment. SARS treats travel between home and your regular place of work as private, and you must keep a logbook of business kilometres to claim a travel deduction (SARS eLogbook 2025/26). If you record business travel for SARS, check that your insurer knows about it too.

If the vehicle is registered to your business, ask your broker whose name the policy should be in. The policyholder, the registered owner and the person who would suffer the loss should line up.

Carrying workers in the load bin

Moving staff in the back of a bakkie is common, but the National Road Traffic Regulations set conditions for it. As reproduced by the Road Freight Association’s FOCUS on Transport (June 2024), from the regulations as at December 2023:

  • Sides high enough. The part of the vehicle carrying people must be enclosed to at least 350 mm above where they sit, or 900 mm above where they stand, strongly enough to stop them falling out (regulation 247).
  • No tools or goods alongside them unless a partition separates the people from the load (regulation 247).
  • Not for reward. You may not carry school children, or any person for reward, in the goods compartment, unless an exemption under the National Land Transport Act applies (regulation 250).

Check the current regulations before you rely on them. Then ask your broker how your policy treats passengers and what liability cover you have if someone riding in the vehicle is injured.

When employees drive

Everyone who drives the vehicle regularly should be declared, and each of them needs a valid licence for that vehicle. Driver details affect the premium and the excesses, and a driver who isn’t properly licensed can put the whole claim at risk.

In an older case reported by FAnews, the insured’s daughter took his car without permission. She held only a learner’s licence and wasn’t accompanied by a licensed driver, as the law requires. The ombud agreed that the policy didn’t provide cover for the collision (FAnews, 2016). The same applies to a new employee who hasn’t got their full licence yet.

Your tools and stock are a separate question

A motor policy is built around the vehicle and the damage it causes. Tools, stock and goods carried in it usually need their own cover. If the bakkie is stolen with a canopy full of equipment, the vehicle and its contents may be dealt with under different sections or different policies, or the contents may not be covered at all.

Depending on what you carry, the cover to ask about might include goods in transit, all-risks cover for portable tools, or the contents section of a business policy. Our goods-in-transit risk checklist helps you work out what’s on board on a typical day.

Business use and bakkie insurance FAQs

General answers about vehicle use classes in South African motor insurance. Your schedule and policy wording decide how your cover works.

What is the difference between private and business use car insurance?

Private use generally covers social and domestic trips and commuting to your regular workplace. Business use adds travel that’s part of your work, such as visiting clients or job sites, and a vehicle insured for business use is also covered for private use. Each insurer’s wording sets the exact boundaries.

What counts as commercial use?

Commercial use generally means the vehicle itself is used to earn an income, for example deliveries, courier work or carrying goods for reward. Short-term Ombud guidance says that even a lift club that leaves the driver with a profit should be disclosed to the insurer as commercial use.

Can my claim be rejected if I used my car for business on a private policy?

It can. In a case described in Short-term Ombud guidance, a claim was rejected because the driver was on his way to a dinner to discuss a business proposition while insured for private use only, and the ombud upheld the rejection.

Does my motor policy cover the tools in my bakkie?

Often not, or only to a limited extent. A motor policy is built around the vehicle. Tools, stock and goods carried in it usually need separate cover, such as goods in transit, all-risks or a business contents section. Check your schedule.

Can I carry workers in the back of my bakkie?

The National Road Traffic Regulations set conditions, including minimum side heights, a partition if tools or goods are carried alongside people, and no carrying people for reward without an exemption. Check the current regulations, and ask your broker how your policy treats passengers.

Do I need to tell my insurer if an employee drives my vehicle?

Yes. Declare everyone who drives the vehicle regularly, and make sure each driver holds a valid licence for that vehicle. Driver details affect the premium, the excesses and whether a claim can be paid.

When should I consider a commercial or fleet policy?

Ask a broker to compare options when the vehicle earns income directly, when it’s owned by your business, or when you run more than one work vehicle. The right answer depends on how the vehicles are used and who drives them.
