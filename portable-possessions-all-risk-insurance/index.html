Portable possessions: what “all risk” cover really means

Your phone, laptop, engagement ring and camera spend most of their lives outside your house, and that’s exactly where your household contents cover stops. Portable possessions insurance, usually called all risk cover, is meant to fill that gap. Here’s how it works in South Africa, the difference between specified and unspecified items, and the conditions that sink claims, especially over December.

Quick answer Portable possessions (all risk) insurance is for belongings you take out of the house, such as phones, laptops, jewellery and cameras, while household contents cover protects your belongings “inside the house” (National Financial Ombud (February 2026)). Valuable items usually need to be specified individually: the Ombud says a phone on a household policy “would have to be specified under the portable possessions section of the policy”, which costs extra premium (NFO (August 2026)). Cover still has conditions, and the sum insured is the most the insurer will pay, not a guaranteed payout.

What portable possessions insurance is

Portable possessions insurance is the section of a personal lines policy that follows your belongings when they leave home. Household contents cover is tied to your address; all risk cover is tied to the item. The Insurance Institute of South Africa describes all risk cover as “designed to protect personal items when insureds leave the country temporarily, for a business trip or holiday” (IISA, Cover (January 2023)), and it applies in South Africa too, from the gym to the beach.

The name “all risk” suggests the cover has no limits. It does have limits, and they matter most when you claim.

What does all risk insurance cover?

All risk cover pays for accidental loss of or damage to an insured item from any cause the policy doesn’t exclude, rather than only from a list of named events such as fire or burglary. That’s broader than contents cover, but it still has exclusions and conditions. The Ombud notes that “most policies exclude cover where the device is left unattended and when the theft is not accompanied by force” (NFO).

Common limits and conditions to look for in your wording include:

  • Unattended items: items left in a car, on a beach towel or at a table may be excluded, or covered only if specific conditions are met.
  • Theft from vehicles: policies may require the car to be locked, the item hidden and signs of forced entry.
  • Wear and tear: gradual damage and depreciation are usually excluded.
  • Loss vs theft: some policies treat simply losing an item differently from theft.
  • Where the item is covered: South Africa only, Southern Africa, or worldwide.

The most important choice you make when you take out the cover is which items to list by name.

Specified vs unspecified items

Specified items are listed one by one on your schedule with a description and value; unspecified items share a general limit. Most policies cap what they pay for any single unspecified item, so a R30 000 laptop or a diamond ring usually needs to be specified to be covered at its full value. Your schedule shows the limits that apply to you.

Specifying an item only helps if the right item is on the schedule. The Ombud’s advice is to “notify your insurer immediately when you replace or upgrade your phone” and, under household contents policies, to “list your phone under portable possessions for proper cover” (NFO). In one case, a claim failed because the complainant had not shown “that the stolen device was on cover”. Standalone phone policies have their own rules: most “will only cover the device when it is used with a specified SIM”, and in another case a tablet snatched through an open car window was rejected because the SIM card was not blacklisted after the robbery.

For jewellery, a current valuation matters because the price moves. The Ombud points out that “jewellery, for example, may increase in value as the price of gold and precious gems rises”, and that consumers should update the sum insured “or risk receiving a lower payout for valuable items” (NFO). Our explainer on what sum insured means covers how these values work. Even with everything listed correctly, the claim still has to meet the policy’s conditions.

Where portable possessions claims go wrong

Most rejected all risk claims come down to how the item was lost and what you can prove. Theft from cars is the clearest example. In a case before the Ombud, a policy required that “the vehicle must be locked, the device must be concealed in the cubby hole or boot, and the loss must be accompanied by forcible and violent entry into the vehicle”. The complainant said he’d locked the car, but “there was no evidence of forcible and violent entry into the vehicle and, therefore, the insurer’s rejection of the claim was upheld” (NFO).

This is a common crime. SAPS recorded 18 068 cases of theft out of or from motor vehicles between October and December 2025, down from 19 409 a year earlier (SAPS crime statistics, Q3 2025/26). Those are the same festive-season months when bags, laptops and phones are most often left in cars outside shops, restaurants and holiday accommodation.

Proof is the other hurdle. “The responsibility lies with the consumer to prove the value of items claimed. This usually requires proof of ownership or purchase”, and gifts or older items without paperwork may be settled at a lower value. Inflated or false documents are worse than none: “even one fraudulent item can invalidate the whole claim and lead to the insurer cancelling the policy” (NFO). Even an approved claim may not pay out what you expect.

How claims are settled: replace, not refund

Insurers usually settle by replacing the item, or paying what it would cost them to replace it, not the price you paid or the value on your certificate. In a case the Ombud published in 2026, a woman’s stolen ring was insured for R155 000 and had a jeweller’s valuation of R125 000. She asked for cash, and the insurer offered R59 030, based on a replacement quote for exactly the same ring from its supplier, plus a refund of the extra premium. The Ombud agreed the insurer “had met its duty to indemnify”, adding that “the sum insured is not a guaranteed payout”: it’s “the maximum the insurer will pay” (NFO).

Devices work the same way. “When a device is lost, insurers typically opt to replace it”, and if the model is outdated, “most policies permit insurers to substitute it with a comparable device, up to the insured value” (NFO). Underinsurance can also reduce a claim: if your contents are insured for less than their replacement value, the insurer may apply average, as explained in our guide to underinsurance and the average clause. Whether all risk cover is worth adding depends on what you carry.

Is portable possessions insurance worth it?

It’s worth considering for anything you carry regularly that would be expensive to replace, and that your contents cover doesn’t reach once it leaves the house. December brings its own risks, so it helps to know what to do the moment something goes missing.

If something is stolen over the holidays

Report the theft to SAPS straight away and get a case number. SABRIC’s guidance is to “visit the nearest police station immediately to report the theft and obtain a case number”, which “will be required for any insurance claims” (SABRIC). For a phone, contact your network to suspend the SIM, and use the device’s tracking feature to locate or lock it. ICASA warns that mobile-related crimes such as SIM-swap fraud increase over the festive season (ICASA, The Focus (December 2025)).

Then notify your insurer as soon as you can, with the case number, proof of ownership and a short account of what happened. Our guide on how to make an insurance claim step by step explains the evidence that helps. Be accurate when you describe the loss and the item: misstating facts is a common reason claims fail, and the Ombud publishes a whole category of decided non-disclosure cases. If your claim is rejected, you can challenge it and then go to the Ombud, whose Non-life Insurance Division handles “Contents and Personal Possessions Insurance” disputes (NFO), as set out in our guide to rejected claims. Key takeaways

  • Household contents cover protects belongings at home; portable possessions (all risk) cover follows items when you take them out.
  • Valuable items usually need to be specified with a value; unspecified items share a general limit, often with a per-item cap.
  • Update your schedule when you replace a phone or laptop, and update jewellery valuations as prices change.
  • Theft from cars is a common claim problem: policies may require the car locked, the item hidden and signs of forced entry.
  • The sum insured is the most an insurer pays; settlement is usually the cost of replacing the item.
  • After a theft, get a SAPS case number, suspend the SIM and keep proof of ownership for the claim.

Portable possessions FAQs

General answers about all risk and portable possessions cover in South Africa.

What is portable possessions insurance?

It is the section of a personal insurance policy, usually called all risk, that covers belongings you take out of the house, such as phones, laptops, jewellery, cameras and bicycles. Household contents cover is tied to your home address; portable possessions cover follows the item, often including trips abroad.

What does all risk insurance cover?

All risk cover pays for accidental loss of or damage to insured items from any cause that the policy doesn’t exclude. It still has conditions, such as exclusions for unattended items, rules for theft from vehicles, wear and tear exclusions and limits on where the cover applies.

What does all risk insurance mean?

It means the policy covers an item against all accidental risks except those it specifically excludes, rather than only against a list of named events such as fire or burglary. Read the exclusions to see what “all” leaves out.

What is the difference between specified and unspecified items?

Specified items are listed individually on your schedule with a description and value. Unspecified items share a general limit, often with a cap on any single item. Valuable items usually need to be specified to be insured at their full value.

Does household contents insurance cover my phone outside the house?

Usually not on its own. The National Financial Ombud says that under a household contents policy, a phone would have to be specified under the portable possessions section, which costs extra premium. Check your schedule to see whether your phone is listed.

Will my insurer pay the full sum insured for a stolen item?

Not necessarily. The sum insured is the maximum the insurer will pay. Insurers usually settle at the cost of replacing the item, which can be lower. In one Ombud case, a ring insured for R155 000 was settled at R59 030, the cost of replacing it with the same ring.

Is my laptop covered if it’s stolen from my car?

It depends on the policy conditions. Many policies cover theft from a vehicle only if the car was locked, the item was hidden in the boot or cubby hole, and there are signs of forced entry. The Ombud upheld a rejection where there was no evidence of forced entry.

What should I do if something is stolen?

Report it to SAPS immediately and get a case number, which insurers require. For a phone, contact your network to suspend the SIM and use the tracking feature. Then notify your insurer with the case number and proof of ownership.
