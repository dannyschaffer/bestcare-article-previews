Household contents insurance in South Africa: what it costs and what drives the price

House contents insurance is priced on risk, not off a price list. Your suburb, your security, your claims history and above all the sum insured you choose set the number. Here’s what drives the premium, how to set the sum insured so a claim isn’t cut short, and where contents claims most often go wrong.

Quick answer There’s no independent, published average premium for house contents insurance in South Africa, because each insurer prices your home on its own risk factors. The biggest lever is the sum insured: it should equal what it would cost to replace everything you own today. If it’s too low, the insurer may apply “average” and reduce your payout (National Financial Ombud). After that, location, security, claims history, the excess you choose and any specified valuables move the price.

What household contents insurance covers, and what it usually leaves out

Household contents insurance protects the movable things in your home, such as furniture, appliances, clothing and jewellery, while buildings insurance deals with the structure itself. The National Financial Ombud and the South African Insurance Association use the same definition: contents cover “protects your personal belongings inside the house”, and it “differs from building insurance, which covers the physical structure of the property” (NFO (2026); SAIA (2026)). The FSCA’s consumer guide adds that “household” means the possessions of the people living with you are generally included too (FSCA (2021)).

If you own your home, you’ll usually need both policies; our guide to buildings vs home contents insurance shows where the line between them falls, and the buildings insurance guide covers the other half.

What a contents policy pays for depends on its wording, but most South African policies are built around insured events such as theft or burglary, fire, storm and water damage, and accidental damage where it’s included. The same policies commonly limit or exclude:

  • Wear and tear. The FSCA lists “normal wear and tear of an item” among common exclusions. It’s also the reason one recent power-surge claim failed, as the rulings below show.
  • Items away from home. Phones, laptops, cameras and jewellery you carry with you normally need to be specified under a separate portable possessions or all-risks section, at an extra premium (NFO (2026)). Our page on portable possessions cover explains how that section works.
  • Theft without forced entry, especially while the home is unoccupied.
  • Cash, outbuildings and garden items, which often carry their own lower limits. The limits differ between insurers, so they’re worth reading on your own schedule.

Those limits matter for price as well as for claims, because every extra you add, and every risk you remove, shows up in the premium.

How much does house contents insurance cost?

Your premium is the insurer’s price for the risk of your particular home, applied to the sum insured you choose, so two neighbours can pay very different amounts. We couldn’t find an independent survey or official statistic that publishes a typical monthly contents premium for South Africa. The figures you’ll see online come from insurers’ own marketing, which is why we don’t quote one here.

What is published is the risk insurers are pricing. South African Police Service figures for the 2025/26 financial year recorded 128,498 burglaries at residential premises, down 7.5% from 138,874 the year before. That’s still about 350 a day, and Gauteng had the most, with 25,979 (SAPS 2025/26 figures via BusinessTech (2026)).

Crime is only one input, though. The next section breaks down the factors you can actually see and change.

What drives your contents premium

Insurers price household contents on the value at risk and the chance of a claim, and most of the factors behind that are visible on your quote and your schedule. Some you can change today; others you can only disclose accurately.

Security deserves a closer look, because it works in two directions. Meeting your insurer’s security requirements can help the price, but a warranty you don’t meet can stop a claim. The NFO’s general advice is to make sure you comply with the security requirements in your policy “in order to have cover in the event of a loss” (NFO). The excess works the same way it does on car cover; our guide to how excesses work explains the rules insurers must follow when they set one.

Of all these factors, the sum insured is both the one you control most and the one that does the most damage when it’s wrong.

Setting the sum insured so a claim isn’t cut short

Set your contents sum insured at what it would cost to replace everything you own today, not what you paid for it and not a round number from years ago. The NFO explains that a settlement is usually “the cost of replacing the item at the time of the loss / damage”: a phone bought in 2022 for R10,000 that now costs R8,000 would be settled at R8,000 (NFO). Our explainer on what the sum insured on your schedule means covers the terms.

The bigger risk runs the other way. If your total sum insured is lower than the real replacement value of your contents, the insurer may apply average and pay only a proportion of each claim, even a small one. The NFO warns that this “may result in a significantly reduced pay-out”, and adds that although insurers may raise premiums each year for inflation, “most policies state it is your responsibility, as the policyholder, to check that all the items on the policy are insured for their correct replacement value.” Our guide to underinsurance and the average clause shows the calculation step by step.

Where contents claims go wrong: five ombud rulings

Most rejected contents claims turn on the policy wording, not on whether the loss happened. These recent rulings from the National Financial Ombud and the FAIS Ombud show the patterns.

  • A brief absence counted as “unoccupied”. A home was burgled while the residents were out for a short time, with no sign of forced entry. The policy required evidence of forced entry for unoccupied premises, and the NFO applied the ordinary meaning of “unoccupied”, so the insurer could reject the claim (NFO (2024)).
  • A new phone that was never added to the policy. A claimant replaced an insured phone with a new one but didn’t update the policy, and the new phone was stolen. Edite Teixeira-McKinon, Lead Ombud of the NFO’s Non-life Insurance Division, said “it was pointed out to the complainant that she had not brought the claim within the ambit of the policy wording by demonstrating that the stolen device was on cover” (NFO (2026)).
  • Movers who never delivered. When a removal company kept a family’s belongings after a move, the insurer called them “missing items” and offered R10 000, its all-risks limit. The NFO found the claimant had shown theft on a balance of probabilities, said “the onus then shifted to the insurer to prove that it was not theft”, and issued a final ruling that the insurer accept liability (NFO (2026)).
  • A power-surge claim that came down to evidence. An insurer’s assessor found a microwave had failed through wear and tear. Under a policy that pays only for listed insured events, the NFO said the claimant then had to show that a surge was the real cause, and the claimant’s report didn’t do that (NFO (2026)). The FAIS Ombud notes separately that surge cover differs between insurers (FAIS Ombud).
  • Jewellery outside the safe. A policy required jewellery worth more than R150 000 to be kept in a locked safe when not worn. Jewellery was taken from a bag in an aircraft’s overhead locker, and a R785,000 claim was reduced to R150,000, with R135,000 paid after the excess (FAIS Ombud (2022)). Higher-value pieces and collections may need specialist cover.

The lesson in each case is the same: the schedule and the wording decide the claim, so check them when something changes, not after a loss. One group is especially likely to assume they’re protected when they aren’t: tenants.

Contents insurance for tenants and renters

If you rent, your landlord’s buildings insurance deals with the structure, not with your belongings, so protecting your furniture, clothes and electronics is up to you. The definitions above draw exactly that line: contents insurance is for personal belongings, buildings insurance for the physical property. A tenant’s contents policy works the same way as a homeowner’s, and the same rules on sum insured, security and specified items apply. If you live in a sectional title complex, our guide to sectional title insurance explains what the body corporate insures and what it doesn’t.

Whether you rent or own, the decision to insure your contents comes down to one question.

Is home contents insurance worth it?

Contents insurance is worth considering if replacing your belongings after a burglary, fire or storm would be more than you could comfortably pay from savings. Add up the replacement value with the calculator above. For many households the total is far higher than expected, because a fully furnished home, its clothing and its appliances add up quickly. A broker can then compare how different insurers would price that sum insured and what conditions they’d attach. Key takeaways

  • Contents insurance protects the movable things in your home; buildings insurance deals with the structure.
  • There’s no independent published average premium: each insurer prices your home’s own risk.
  • The sum insured is the biggest lever. Set it at today’s replacement cost, or average may cut your payout.
  • Location, security, claims history, the excess and specified valuables also move the price.
  • Security warranties can stop a claim if they aren’t met, so know exactly what yours require.
  • Phones and jewellery you carry need to be specified, and updated when you replace them.
  • Tenants need their own contents cover; the landlord’s policy deals with the building.

House contents insurance FAQs

General answers about household contents insurance in South Africa. Your policy schedule and wording set the specific terms of your cover.

What does household contents insurance cover?

It protects the movable things in your home, such as furniture, appliances, clothing and jewellery, against the insured events your policy lists, commonly including theft, fire, storm and water damage. The structure of the home falls under buildings insurance. Limits and exclusions differ between policies, so check your schedule and wording.

How much does house contents insurance cost in South Africa?

There’s no independent published average. Each insurer prices your home on its own risk factors, applied to the sum insured you choose. The main factors are the sum insured, where you live, your security, your claims history, the excess and any specified valuables.

How do I work out how much contents cover I need?

Add up what it would cost to replace everything you own today, room by room, including the garage, garden and storage areas. The National Financial Ombud recommends taking an inventory and comparing it with the insured value on your coversheet.

What happens if my contents are underinsured?

If your sum insured is lower than the real replacement value of your contents, the insurer may apply average and pay only a proportion of your claim. The National Financial Ombud warns that this may significantly reduce the payout.

Does house contents insurance cover my phone?

A phone you carry outside the home usually needs to be specified under the portable possessions or all-risks section, at an extra premium. If you replace the phone, update the policy: the NFO has upheld a rejection where a new phone was never added.

Do tenants need contents insurance?

A landlord’s buildings insurance deals with the structure, not the tenant’s belongings. Tenants who want their furniture, clothing and electronics protected need their own contents policy.

Will an alarm or burglar bars lower my premium?

Security is one of the factors insurers weigh for theft risk, and it can affect the price. Policies may also contain security warranties, such as an alarm that must be set, and a warranty you don’t meet can affect a claim. Check exactly what your policy requires.

Does contents insurance pay out the price I originally paid?

Usually not. The National Financial Ombud explains that a settlement is typically the cost of replacing the item at the time of the loss, which may be lower than what you originally paid.
