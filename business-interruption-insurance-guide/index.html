Business interruption insurance: what actually triggers a payout

Business interruption insurance is meant to keep a business alive while it can’t trade. But most disputes aren’t about how much was lost. They’re about whether the cover was triggered at all. Here’s what starts a claim, what the extensions add, what the COVID-19 court cases decided, and how the sum insured is worked out.

Quick answer Business interruption insurance pays for the gross profit you lose, and some extra costs of keeping going, after an insured event stops or slows trading. The standard trigger is insured physical damage at your premises: in a published ombud case, a business that lost sales in the 2022 KwaZulu-Natal floods had its claim turned down because “there was no damage to the building” (Ombudsman for Short-term Insurance, 2022 Annual Report). Extensions for suppliers, customers, utilities and prevention of access can widen the trigger, but each has its own conditions and often a shorter indemnity period.

What business interruption insurance pays for

Business interruption insurance replaces the income a business loses while it recovers from insured damage, so it can keep paying rent, salaries and loan repayments until trading is back to normal. It sits next to your commercial property cover, which pays to repair the building and replace stock and equipment, but doesn’t pay for the months of lost trade that follow.

What triggers business interruption insurance

In a standard wording, business interruption cover is triggered only when there is insured physical damage at your premises, the insurer has paid or accepted that damage under a property section, and the damage caused the interruption. All three steps have to be there. A drop in turnover on its own, however serious, isn’t enough.

The Ombudsman for Short-term Insurance (now part of the National Financial Ombud) published a clear example. After the April 2022 floods in KwaZulu-Natal, a home industry business lost electricity and water, the roads were “either washed away or completely flooded”, and it couldn’t make deliveries. It claimed for lost sales and stock under the business interruption section. The policy covered loss “in consequence of damage occurring during the period of insurance at the premises”, and the insurer argued “there was no damage to the building and, therefore, the business interruption section of the policy was not triggered”. The ombud agreed that the loss described “did not meet the criteria for cover” (OSTI 2022 Annual Report, pp. 81 to 82).

The three questions a business interruption claim has to answer. Was there damage at the insured premises? Has the insurer paid or accepted liability for that damage under a listed property section? Did that damage cause the interruption? If the answer to the first is no, look to the extensions.

This is also why your property cover matters to your income cover. If a fire or storm claim is reduced or rejected under the property section, for example because of underinsurance and the average clause, the business interruption claim that depends on it can be affected too. The extensions are where the trigger moves beyond your own four walls.

Cover when the damage isn’t at your premises

Extensions let a business interruption claim start from damage somewhere else, or from certain events with no damage at all. They are optional, they carry their own limits, and they are where wording differences matter most.

  • Suppliers’ and customers’ premises. Damage at a key supplier or customer that stops your trade. Some wordings name the suppliers; others cover unnamed ones up to a lower limit.
  • Public utilities. Loss of electricity, water or telecommunications supply. Read the exclusions carefully: insurers have had policy wording drafted specifically for “the exclusion of coverage relative to grid failure / grid interruption related losses (including consequential losses)” (ENS, insurance practice brochure). Load-shedding is a question to ask, not an assumption to make.
  • Prevention of access. Damage or an incident nearby that stops customers or staff reaching your premises. Check whether access must be completely prevented or only hindered, and how far away the event may be.
  • Infectious or notifiable disease. Closure after a disease outbreak at or near the premises. This is the extension the COVID-19 cases were fought over.
  • Riot, strike and unrest. These special risks are handled by Sasria, which offers a business interruption policy that attaches to your underlying cover. Sasria’s wording says you must have “an underlying policy contract in force that includes Sasria cover at the time of the event” (Sasria Business Interruption wording (2026)).

Each extension can have a shorter indemnity period than the main section, which is exactly what one of the biggest South African business interruption cases turned on.

What the COVID-19 business interruption cases decided

The COVID-19 cases didn’t create a general right to be paid for the lockdown. They decided what particular infectious disease clauses meant, and they show how much a single sentence in a wording can be worth.

At the start, the regulator took a narrow view. In June 2020 the Financial Sector Conduct Authority said it had “found no evidence in support of the view that the National lockdown could be a trigger for a valid BI insurance cover claim”, but that insurers whose wordings fell into its radius, notification or notifiable disease categories must not delay paying claims that met those requirements. For wordings with a general exclusion, it said there was “no sound basis” to direct insurers to pay (FSCA Communication 34 of 2020 (INS)).

The courts then looked at the wording in front of them:

  • Guardrisk v Café Chameleon (SCA, December 2020). The policy covered interruption due to a notifiable disease within 50 kilometres of the premises. The Supreme Court of Appeal held that the insured event included both the outbreak and the government’s response to it, so the lockdown losses fell within the clause ([2020] ZASCA 173).
  • Santam v Ma-Afrika Hotels (SCA, October 2021). The fight was over how long cover lasted. The schedule gave the business interruption section an 18-month indemnity period, while a note limited extensions to three months. The court held that the disease cover carried the 18-month period and dismissed the insurer’s appeal ([2021] ZASCA 141).
  • AIG v PTC Aviation (SCA, June 2024). The court applied the same approach to causation, but one claimant still failed because it wasn’t an insured under the policy and hadn’t met the claim notification conditions ([2024] ZASCA 97).

The practical lesson is the same for any business today: the trigger, the radius, the indemnity period for each extension and the claim conditions are the terms that decide a claim. They are worth reading before something goes wrong. Getting the sum insured right is the other half.

How is business interruption insurance calculated?

The sum insured is usually based on the gross profit the business expects to earn over the indemnity period, adjusted for growth. If the indemnity period is longer than 12 months, the annual figure is scaled up to match it, so an 18-month period needs roughly one and a half years of gross profit.

Getting it wrong costs money both ways. Too high, and you pay premium for cover you can’t use. Too low, and most wordings apply average, cutting the payout in proportion to the shortfall, in the same way as on a property claim. When you choose the indemnity period, think about the slowest part of recovery, not the fastest. Rebuilding may take nine months, but replacing specialised machinery, re-registering a site or winning back contracts can take far longer. Once the cover is sized, the next question is what happens when you actually claim.

How to claim on business interruption insurance

Notify the insurer as soon as the damage happens, not when the losses have been added up. The business interruption claim follows the property claim, and late notice can give an insurer a reason to dispute it, as the claimant in the AIG case found.

  1. Report the damage straight away and ask whether a loss adjuster will be appointed for both the property and income parts of the claim.
  2. Keep trading if you can. Reasonable steps that reduce the loss, such as moving to temporary premises, are usually part of what the cover expects. Keep every receipt.
  3. Gather the numbers: monthly management accounts for at least the last two years, the latest annual financial statements, VAT returns and order books, so the loss can be measured against your normal trend.
  4. Agree how the loss will be measured before arguing about the amount: the period, the trend adjustment and which costs have stopped.
  5. Ask about interim payments during a long claim, so cash flow doesn’t run out while the final figure is worked out.

The insurer has duties too. Under the Policyholder Protection Rules it must give written notice of its decision within 10 days of making it, and a rejection must come with reasons and at least 90 days for you to make representations (Policyholder Protection Rules (Short-term), rule 17.6). Business interruption isn’t only about fire and floods. A ransomware attack can stop trading just as completely, and that risk is usually handled by cyber insurance rather than the property-linked section. Key takeaways

  • Business interruption insurance replaces lost gross profit and some extra costs after an insured event; property cover doesn’t do this.
  • The standard trigger is insured physical damage at your premises. Lost trade without that damage usually isn’t enough.
  • Extensions for suppliers, customers, utilities, prevention of access and disease widen the trigger, each with its own limit and often a shorter indemnity period.
  • Load-shedding and grid failure can be excluded or limited, so ask before relying on a utilities extension.
  • Riot and unrest losses need Sasria’s business interruption cover on the schedule.
  • The COVID-19 judgments turned on the exact wording, including an 18 month versus 3 month indemnity period.
  • Size the sum insured on gross profit over the full indemnity period, or average can cut the payout.

Business interruption insurance FAQs

General answers about business interruption insurance in South Africa.

What does business interruption insurance cover?

It is designed to replace the gross profit a business loses, and pay certain extra costs of keeping it going, after an insured event disrupts trading. What is included depends on the basis of cover, the sum insured, the indemnity period and the extensions on your schedule.

What triggers business interruption insurance?

The standard trigger is insured physical damage at your premises, such as a fire or storm damage that the insurer has paid or accepted under the property section. Without that damage, the main business interruption section usually doesn’t respond. Extensions can widen the trigger, for example to a supplier’s premises or a prevention of access event.

Does business interruption insurance cover load-shedding or power cuts?

Not automatically. Loss of electricity without damage at your premises usually falls outside the main business interruption section, and some wordings exclude grid failure altogether. Ask whether your policy has a public utilities extension and exactly what it excludes.

How is business interruption insurance calculated?

The sum insured is usually based on gross profit, broadly turnover less the costs that stop when trading stops, projected over the indemnity period and adjusted for expected growth. If the sum insured is too low, the payout can be reduced under the average clause.

What is the indemnity period in business interruption insurance?

It is the maximum period after the damage during which lost gross profit is covered, commonly 12, 18 or 24 months. It should be long enough to rebuild, replace equipment and win back customers. Extensions can carry shorter indemnity periods than the main section, so check each one.

Do I need business interruption insurance if I have property insurance?

Property insurance pays to repair or replace damaged buildings, stock and equipment. It doesn’t replace the income lost while the business can’t trade. Business interruption insurance is a separate section that has to be selected and sized.

Are riots and unrest covered by business interruption insurance?

Business interruption losses caused by riot, strike, civil commotion, public disorder or terrorism are a special risk handled by Sasria, which offers a business interruption policy that attaches to your underlying cover. Check that Sasria cover is on your schedule.

How do I claim on business interruption insurance?

Notify the insurer as soon as possible, keep trading records, management accounts and receipts for extra costs, and agree how the loss will be measured. Insurers commonly appoint a loss adjuster. If a claim is rejected you can make representations and then complain to the National Financial Ombud.
