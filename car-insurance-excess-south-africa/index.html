What is an insurance excess, and can you lower it?

The excess is the part of a claim you pay yourself. Here’s how it works in South Africa, the extra excesses that catch people out, and the ways to bring it down, with their trade-offs.

Quick answer An insurance excess, also called the first amount payable, is the part of a valid claim you pay or bear yourself. The insurer pays the rest. You usually pay it whether or not the accident was your fault. Policies can add extra excesses on top of the basic one, for example for younger or newly licensed drivers, or for theft and hijacking. You can usually lower your excess by accepting a higher premium, or by paying extra for an excess waiver, which may only reduce the basic excess (FAIS Ombud, 2016).

What an insurance excess means

The excess is the amount you carry yourself when you claim. The insurer pays the approved claim minus the excess. The Policyholder Protection Rules define excesses as “amounts payable or borne by policyholders in the event of claims or losses under a policy”. The FAIS Ombud describes it as “the first amount payable in the event of a claim, and is the portion for which you are self-insured”.

How the excess works on a claim Approved claim − your excess(es) = what the insurer pays Example: R30,000 of approved repairs with a R5,000 excess means the insurer pays R25,000.

A basic excess is often a percentage of the claim with a minimum amount. The FAIS Ombud’s 2016 example was 5% of the claim with a minimum of R2,500. Your own figures are on your policy schedule. When a claim is settled in cash, for example after a car is stolen or written off, applicable excesses can be deducted from the settlement rather than paid separately.

The basic excess is rarely the whole story, though.

The extra excesses that can apply

Many motor policies add extra excesses for particular drivers or circumstances, and these are added to the basic excess, not instead of it. The FAIS Ombud put it plainly: “All these excesses are cumulative in addition to the basic excess.”

Additional motor excesses found in the South African market
Type of excessWhen it may apply
Young driverThe driver at the time is under a set age, often 25. One case involved a policy that applied it to drivers under 30
Newly licensed driverThe driver has held a licence for less than a set period, such as a year
Theft or hijackingThe car is stolen or hijacked
Time of accidentThe accident happens at certain hours, for example between midnight and 5am
Driver not on the policySomeone other than a listed or nominated driver was driving
Early claimThe claim happens within a set period of the policy starting, such as six months
Voluntary excessAn amount you choose to add in exchange for a lower premium

Not every policy has all of these, and the amounts vary. The FAIS Ombud gave an example of how they stack: a driver under 25, licensed for less than a year, who has an accident at 2am “could be liable for a significant portion of the claim”.

One FAIS Ombud case shows why excesses deserve attention whenever cover changes. A policyholder’s new car was moved onto a new policy with a different insurer, instead of being added to her existing one. When her 25-year-old son, the nominated driver, had a loss, she found she owed double the excess: the new policy added excesses for drivers under 30 and for claims within six months of the policy starting. The provider offered to compensate her for the additional excesses, and she accepted.

Do you pay the excess if the accident wasn’t your fault?

Usually, yes. The excess is a term of your contract with your own insurer, so it applies no matter who caused the accident. You may get it back later, but that isn’t guaranteed.

  • Recovery is the insurer’s choice. If someone else caused the loss, the insurer may try to recover its costs from them or their insurer. When it does, it usually includes your excess. The former Short-term Insurance Ombud said “there is no obligation on an insurer to recover the excess paid” (Moonstone).
  • A successful recovery normally means a refund. If the insurer recovers the full loss, including the excess, it will reimburse your excess. The NFO’s Lead Ombud has said insurers often refund it even after only a partial recovery.
  • You can pursue the other party yourself. If the insurer decides not to pursue recovery, nothing stops you bringing your own civil claim against the person who caused the accident.

In a case described by the NFO’s Lead Ombud, a claimant complained that his insurer hadn’t yet recovered his excess from the driver at fault. The NFO found in favour of the insurer. It had started the recovery, the process was still under way, and the NFO couldn’t compel it to recover the excess.

How to lower your excess, and the trade-offs

You can usually reduce what you’d pay at claim time, but it generally costs more in premium, and some options have limits. These are the main routes:

Choose a lower excess for a higher premium

Excess and premium move in opposite directions. Most insurers reduce the premium if you choose a higher excess, and “for higher premiums you can lower your excesses” (Smart About Money, reviewed by the NFO’s Lead Ombud). A voluntary excess works the other way: a lower premium now, a bigger bill when you claim.

Ask about an excess waiver or buy-back

Some insurers offer a product that reduces or removes the excess for an additional premium. Check its scope: in the FAIS Ombud’s example, the waiver applied only to the basic excess, and the additional excesses still applied.

List every regular driver

Naming everyone who drives the car regularly avoids excesses for drivers who aren’t on the policy. It also protects the claim itself, because a misrepresented regular driver can lead to a rejected claim. Our guide to how car insurance premiums are calculated describes an ombud case where that happened.

Know what triggers the extras

Read the excess section of your schedule for the conditions that add to it, such as who is driving, the time of day or how soon after inception you claim. Knowing them won’t always let you avoid them, but it avoids surprises.

What insurers must tell you about excesses

Insurers must disclose every excess that may apply, when it applies and what happens if you don’t pay it, and they must keep excesses fair and reasonable. The Policyholder Protection Rules require:

  • details of “any excesses that may become payable by the policyholder, the circumstances under which it will be payable and the consequences of not paying” before you take the policy, and confirmation in writing after it starts (Rules 11.4.2(d)(iii) and 11.5.1)
  • disclosure of the excess payable when you claim (Rule 17.8.3(d))
  • that the excess “must be fair and reasonable” and “may not constitute an unreasonable barrier to a claimant” (Rule 17.10.1)

Advisers have duties too. In another FAIS Ombud case, a policyholder’s claim settlement was reduced by excesses she said had never been discussed. The adviser’s record mentioned the excesses, but not their extent, or that an additional excess would apply if the car was stolen. The Ombud’s office found she couldn’t have made an informed decision before buying, and the provider settled her wider complaint, which also concerned credit shortfall cover, for R80,337.

Excesses aren’t only a motor issue. They also apply to home contents and buildings cover. In a sectional title scheme, owners pay the excess on damage to parts they must maintain, such as their geyser. Our guide to sectional title insurance explains how that works.

Key takeaways

  • The excess, or first amount payable, is the part of a valid claim you pay yourself.
  • You usually pay it whether or not the accident was your fault.
  • Additional excesses, for example for young or new drivers, theft or late-night accidents, can stack on top of the basic excess.
  • Insurers don’t have to recover your excess from the other party, but normally refund it if they recover the full loss.
  • A lower excess usually means a higher premium. An excess waiver may only cover the basic excess.
  • Insurers must disclose every excess, and excesses must be fair and reasonable.

Insurance excess FAQs

General answers about excesses on South African short-term insurance. Your policy schedule and wording set your actual excesses.

What does excess mean in insurance?

The excess, also called the first amount payable, is the part of a valid claim you pay or bear yourself. The insurer pays the approved claim minus the excess. The Policyholder Protection Rules define excesses as amounts payable or borne by policyholders in the event of claims or losses under a policy.

Do I pay the excess if the accident wasn’t my fault?

Usually, yes. The excess is part of your contract with your own insurer, so it applies regardless of who caused the accident. If your insurer recovers the full loss from the other party, it will normally refund your excess.

Can I get my excess back from the other driver?

If your insurer pursues a recovery, it usually includes your excess and refunds it if the recovery succeeds. It isn’t obliged to pursue the excess, and the ombud can’t compel it to. If the insurer doesn’t pursue recovery, you can bring your own civil claim against the person who caused the accident.

What is an excess waiver or excess buy-back?

It’s an option, offered by some insurers for an additional premium, that reduces or removes the excess. Check exactly which excesses it covers. In a FAIS Ombud example, the waiver applied only to the basic excess, so the additional excesses still applied.

Does a higher excess lower my premium?

Generally, yes. Most insurers reduce the premium if you choose a higher excess, and charge a higher premium for a lower one. The trade-off is what you would pay yourself on each claim.

Can more than one excess apply to the same claim?

Yes. Additional excesses, for example for a young or newly licensed driver, theft or hijacking, or an accident late at night, can be added to the basic excess on the same claim. Your schedule lists which ones apply.

Does home insurance have an excess too?

Yes. Excesses apply to most types of short-term insurance, including home contents and buildings cover. In a sectional title scheme, owners pay the excess on damage to parts of the building they must maintain, such as the geyser serving their unit.
