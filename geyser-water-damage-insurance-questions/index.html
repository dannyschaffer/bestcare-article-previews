Is your geyser covered? What South African homeowners should know before it bursts

A burst geyser is one of the most common home insurance claims, and one of the most commonly disputed. Whether you’re paid usually comes down to three questions: what failed, what it damaged, and why it failed.

Quick answer Many buildings policies include a geyser that bursts suddenly and the water damage it causes, less your excess. What varies is cover for wear and tear, rust, slow leaks and parts such as valves and thermostats. Bursting water apparatus, such as geysers and pipes, made up 15% of the homeowners’ complaints the National Financial Ombud finalised in 2025, and the most common reason for homeowners’ complaints was a claim rejected for gradual deterioration, lack of maintenance or wear and tear (NFO Annual Report 2025).

The three questions behind every geyser claim

Policies often treat the geyser, the damage it causes and the reason it failed as separate questions, each with its own conditions. Knowing which one you’re dealing with tells you what to ask.

1. The geyser itselfReplacing the cylinder after it bursts. Parts such as the element, thermostat, valves and drip tray may be treated differently from the cylinder.
2. The resultant damageCeilings, walls and floors are usually a buildings question. Furniture, carpets and belongings may be a contents claim with its own excess.
3. The causeA sudden burst is the kind of event insurance is for. Rust, corrosion and a slow leak over weeks may fall under a wear and tear exclusion.
4. Your excessMany policies have a specific geyser excess. If the contents are damaged too, a second excess may apply.

The cause is where most disputes start.

Sudden burst or wear and tear?

Insurers commonly exclude damage that happens gradually, and they have to prove that exclusion with evidence. We couldn’t find a published ombud case about a disputed geyser claim on its merits. The closest is a 2023 case about a leaking toilet cistern that damaged a wooden floor. The insurer showed, using its service provider’s report, that the damage was caused by wear and tear and had happened over a period of time, and the ombud upheld the rejection (OSTI Annual Report 2023). The same reasoning can apply to a geyser that has been weeping for months.

Some policies include geyser wear and tear, or offer it as an optional extra, and some don’t. Ask your insurer or broker these questions before you need to claim:

  • Does my policy pay to replace a geyser that fails through rust or age, or only one that bursts suddenly?
  • Are the element, thermostat, valves and drip tray included, or only the cylinder?
  • Is water damage from a slow leak treated differently from a burst?
  • What’s my geyser excess, and does a second excess apply to contents?
  • Do I have to use the insurer’s plumber, and what happens in an after-hours emergency?

How the geyser was installed can matter as much as its age.

Installation rules, certificates and who may do the work

Electric geysers are installed to SANS 10254, the national standard for fixed electric storage water heaters. In Johannesburg, the water services by-laws require all fixed electric storage water heaters to comply with it, and only a qualified plumber may replace a geyser or its protective devices (City of Johannesburg Water Services By-laws, sections 37 and 39).

  • Drip tray. A drip tray is required where water leaking from the geyser or its connections could cause consequential damage, such as in a roof space above a ceiling (Plumbing Africa, July 2025).
  • Plumbing certificate. The requirement to issue a certificate of compliance for electric geyser installations was removed from SANS 10254 on 24 June 2022 (PIRB, October 2022). But under PIRB’s own rules, a PIRB-licensed plumber must still issue one for geyser work (PIRB), and some municipalities and insurers ask for one. Keep it with your invoice.
  • Electrical work. The plumbers’ and electrical contractors’ associations say plumbers “cannot legally disconnect, remove or reconnect electrical work” at a geyser, and that a registered electrician must issue a supplementary electrical certificate when a geyser or thermostat is replaced (IOPSA and ECA, August 2024). The Electrical Installation Regulations say anyone who does electrical installation work must ensure a valid certificate is issued for it (regulation 9(4)).
Why it matters for a claim: invoices, certificates and photos of the installation show who did the work and when. If the cause of a failure is disputed, that record is evidence, and it’s much easier to keep it from the start than to reconstruct it later.

Solar geysers, heat pumps and power surges

Solar water heaters and heat pumps raise their own questions, and so do surges after load shedding.

  • New homes and additions. The National Building Regulations require at least 50% of a building’s annual hot water to come from means other than electrical resistance heating, such as solar or a heat pump. This applies to new buildings and extensions that need approved plans (regulation XA2, 2011).
  • Hail. The City of Cape Town lists hail as a drawback of evacuated-tube solar collectors, which are “more likely to break from hail and other environmental conditions” (City of Cape Town, 2024). Ask whether roof-mounted collectors are listed on your buildings policy, and how hail damage to them is treated. Our buildings insurance guide covers storm damage more broadly.
  • Surges. The FAIS Ombud says load shedding and blackouts “are not in themselves an insurable risk”, and that surge cover varies: some insurers include it, others offer it as an add-on, and some require surge protectors (FAIS Ombud). Ask whether a surge-damaged element or thermostat would be paid, and under which section.

When it bursts: the first hour

Cut the power, cut the water, record the damage and call your insurer.

Switch off the geyser at the distribution board

Turn off the geyser circuit before going near the water. If the circuits aren’t labelled, switch off the main supply.

Close the water supply

Close the isolating valve on the cold supply to the geyser, or the main stopcock if you can’t reach it. Opening a hot tap releases pressure. The water may still be very hot.

Photograph everything

The NFO advises photos or videos “that clearly show the nature, cause and extent of the damage”, so include the geyser, the drip tray, the ceiling and any damaged belongings (NFO, Non-Life Insurance Tips).

Call your insurer or broker before replacing the geyser

The NFO warns that delay “may affect the result of your claim”. Your insurer may use its own plumbers or want to see the old geyser, so ask before you authorise a replacement.

Tip: ask the plumber to note the cause of failure and the serial numbers of the old and new geysers on the invoice.

A replacement isn’t cheap. An estate agent quoted by Property24 in July 2026 put the cost of replacing a standard geyser at between R8,000 and R15,000, including installation (Property24, July 2026). Your excess comes off any claim, so knowing the amount in advance avoids a surprise. Our excess guide explains how excesses work.

Key takeaways

  • Many buildings policies include a sudden geyser burst and the resulting water damage, less an excess.
  • Wear and tear, rust, slow leaks and parts are where policies differ most, so ask before you need to claim.
  • Water damage to belongings may be a separate contents claim with its own excess.
  • A drip tray, a qualified plumber and a registered electrician reduce the damage and leave a paper trail.
  • In a burst, switch off the power first, then the water, take photos and call your insurer before replacing the geyser.

Geyser insurance FAQs

General answers about geyser claims in South Africa. Your policy schedule and wording decide how your cover works.

Does buildings insurance cover a burst geyser?

Many buildings policies include a geyser that bursts suddenly, and the water damage it causes, subject to an excess. Cover for wear and tear, rust, leaks that develop slowly and parts such as valves and thermostats varies, so check your wording.

Is a geyser claim under buildings or contents insurance?

The geyser is part of the building, so it’s usually a buildings claim. Water damage to your furniture and belongings may fall under your contents policy, which can mean a separate claim and excess.

Will insurance pay if my geyser burst because it was old?

It depends on the policy. Insurers commonly exclude wear and tear and gradual deterioration, and the most common reason homeowners complain to the National Financial Ombud is a claim rejected on that exclusion. Some policies include or offer cover for geyser wear and tear, so ask.

Do I need a certificate of compliance for a new geyser?

Since June 2022, SANS 10254 no longer requires a plumbing certificate for electric geysers, but PIRB-licensed plumbers must issue one under PIRB’s rules, and some municipalities and insurers ask for one. Electrical work on the geyser needs a registered electrician and an electrical certificate.

Who pays for a burst geyser in a rented house?

Usually the landlord, who owns the geyser and normally insures the building. Check your lease. The tenant should report the burst straight away and claims for their own damaged belongings on their contents policy, if they have one.

Who pays for a burst geyser in a sectional title unit?

The geyser is common property insured by the body corporate, but the owner must maintain, repair and replace the geyser serving their unit. Check your scheme’s rules and the body corporate policy for how the excess and resultant damage are handled.

Does insurance cover a geyser damaged by a power surge?

Load shedding itself isn’t an insured event. Power surge cover varies: some policies include it, some offer it as an add-on, and some require surge protection. Ask whether surge damage to the geyser’s element and thermostat is included.
