Cyber insurance for small South African businesses: what it covers and what it asks of you

Cyber insurance in South Africa has moved from a big-company product to a small-business question. A single fraudulent email or locked server can stop trading, expose client data and start a POPIA clock. Here’s what a cyber policy usually pays for, what it leaves out, and what insurers expect from you in return.

Quick answer Cyber insurance pays two kinds of cost after an attack or data breach: your own (forensics, legal advice, data restoration, notifying people, extortion response and lost income while systems are down) and claims made against you by people whose data was exposed. Policies are “generally offered as cover for a combination of first party costs ... and financial losses caused by business interruption, as well as third party liability claims resulting from a cyber-event” (ENS (2021)). Cover depends heavily on the security controls you declare and on following the insurer’s steps when an incident happens.

Why small businesses are in the firing line

South Africa is the most targeted country in Africa for the two attacks that hurt small businesses most: ransomware and business email compromise. Interpol’s 2026 African Cyberthreat Assessment found South Africa accounted for 92% of all ransomware detections on the continent (MyBroadband, reporting Interpol (2026)), and that “70% of BEC detections in Africa originated in South Africa”. Business email compromise “has become the most common type of cyber attack affecting South African organisations” (MyBroadband (2026)).

The data side is growing too. The Information Regulator received 2 374 security compromise notifications under POPIA in 2024/25, a 37% increase on the year before (Information Regulator Annual Report 2024/25). And when a breach happens, the costs add up quickly: IBM put the average cost of a data breach for South African organisations at R44.1 million in 2025 (ITWeb, reporting IBM (2025)). That figure is an average across larger organisations, but the cost items, from investigators and lawyers to downtime, are the same at any size.

Most of these incidents start with a person, not a machine: a convincing email, a reused password or a fake invoice. That shapes both what cyber insurance pays for and what it asks of you.

How cyber insurance works

A cyber policy has two halves. First-party cover pays your own costs of dealing with an incident; third-party cover pays claims others make against you because of it. When you report a suspected incident, usually through a 24-hour line, the insurer’s appointed specialists help contain it, investigate and handle notifications, and covered costs are paid up to the limits on your schedule.

Ordinary business policies often don’t fill the gap: as ENS put it recently, “standard policies often exclude cyber unless an extension or standalone policy is in place” (ENS (2026)). The regulatory line in that table matters most to South African businesses because of POPIA.

POPIA, breach notification and fines

If personal information your business holds is accessed by someone who shouldn’t have it, POPIA requires you to tell the Information Regulator and the people affected. Section 22 says the notification “must be made as soon as reasonably possible after the discovery of the compromise” (POPIA, section 22). That means legal advice and, often, contacting hundreds or thousands of clients, which is why “cyber insurance coverage typically covers the costs incurred during the notification process” (ENS, Privacy in Brief).

The Regulator can impose an administrative fine that may “not exceed R10 million” (POPIA, section 109). Whether insurance can pay it is less certain than many sales pitches suggest. Lawyers at ENS say “administrative fines may be insurable in some circumstances, but criminal fines generally are not”, and that policies cover fines only “where legally insurable and not excluded” (ENS (2026)). Treat fines cover as a possible bonus, not the reason to buy.

The Regulator is enforcing, not just collecting reports. In 2024/25 it issued five enforcement notices after investigations and assessments, one of them to a medical laboratory group (Information Regulator Annual Report 2024/25). Speed matters for the insurance claim as well, because the policy conditions are where most cyber disputes start.

What cyber insurance usually excludes, and what it expects

Cyber policies pay for sudden attacks on a business that was doing what it said it would. The common limits fall into three groups: what you declared, what you do when an incident happens, and events too big for any insurer to carry.

  • Your declared controls. Underwriters typically ask about multi-factor authentication, backups, software updates and staff training before they quote. If the answers on the proposal weren’t accurate, a claim can be disputed. Our guide on how to compare insurance policies covers why disclosure matters on any policy.
  • Steps after an incident. “An insurer might prescribe certain steps to be take[n] when an incident occurs and failure to comply might result in an insurer repudiating a claim” (ENS, Privacy in Brief). Paying a ransom, wiping systems or appointing your own IT firm before calling the insurer can all cause problems.
  • War and state-backed attacks. Since 31 March 2023, Lloyd’s has required standalone cyber policies written in its market to exclude losses from “any state backed cyber-attack” and from war (Lloyd’s Market Bulletin Y5381 (2022)). Ask whether your policy has a similar war or state-backed attack exclusion.
  • Payment diversion and invoice fraud. When staff are tricked into paying a fraudster’s account, the loss may be excluded, sub-limited or offered only as a social engineering extension. Ask about it by name.
  • Prior incidents and betterment. Attacks that started before the policy began, and the cost of upgrading systems beyond what you had, are commonly outside cover.
When you authorise the payment yourself. A consumer downloaded an app linked from a fake airline ticket advert. It contained malware, and R120 000 left her account in two transactions approved with her own selfie authentication. She reported it to her bank and the police 27 minutes later, but the money was gone. The National Financial Ombud found that “by downloading the fraudulent app, the consumer essentially handed over her phone” and couldn’t hold the bank liable (NFO, November 2025). Businesses face the same problem with fake invoices: if the payment was authorised, getting it back depends on the cover you bought for that specific risk.

None of these exclusions are reasons to avoid cyber cover. They are reasons to prepare before you apply.

What to do in the first hours of a cyber incident

Call the insurer’s incident line before you do anything that changes the evidence or commits money. ENS lists the core of a response plan as knowing who is involved, assessing the risk, containing the incident, an initial assessment, remediation, and “a clear understanding of notification obligations, including any notification to insurers” (ENS).

  1. Disconnect affected devices from the network, but don’t switch them off or wipe them.
  2. Report the incident to the insurer and follow its instructions on forensic specialists and any extortion demand.
  3. Phone your bank immediately if money has left an account; speed gives the best chance of stopping the funds.
  4. Open a case with SAPS for fraud or extortion.
  5. With legal advice, notify the Information Regulator and affected people as soon as reasonably possible if personal information was compromised.
  6. Keep a written timeline of what happened and when, with copies of emails, invoices and screenshots.

A cyber attack that stops trading is also a business interruption question, but the standard business interruption section is usually tied to physical damage, so lost income from an attack is normally a cyber policy matter. Key takeaways

  • Cyber insurance combines first-party costs (forensics, restoration, notification, extortion, lost income) with liability to people whose data was exposed.
  • South Africa accounted for 92% of Africa’s ransomware detections and 70% of its email compromise detections in Interpol’s 2026 assessment.
  • POPIA section 22 requires notification as soon as reasonably possible; administrative fines can reach R10 million and are only sometimes insurable.
  • Claims can fail on inaccurate security declarations or on not following the insurer’s incident steps.
  • Invoice and payment diversion fraud is often excluded or sub-limited, so ask about it by name.
  • Ordinary business policies often exclude cyber, and standard business interruption cover is tied to physical damage.

Cyber insurance FAQs

General answers about cyber insurance for South African businesses.

What does cyber insurance cover?

Cyber insurance commonly combines first-party cover for your own costs, such as forensic investigation, legal advice, data restoration, breach notification, cyber extortion and business interruption, with third-party cover for claims against you by clients or others whose data was exposed. The exact cover depends on the policy wording and the sections you choose.

How does cyber insurance work?

You report a suspected incident to the insurer, usually through a hotline, and its panel of responders helps contain the attack, investigate it and meet notification duties. The insurer then pays covered costs and losses up to the limits on your schedule, after the excess. Many policies require you to follow set steps when an incident happens.

Does my business need cyber insurance if we’re small?

Size doesn’t make a business safe. Business email compromise has become the most common type of cyber attack affecting South African organisations, and small firms often have fewer controls. Whether cyber insurance suits your business depends on the data you hold, how dependent you are on IT systems, and what you could absorb yourself.

Does cyber insurance pay POPIA fines?

Sometimes. POPIA allows administrative fines of up to R10 million. Legal commentary says administrative fines may be insurable in some circumstances, while criminal fines generally are not, and policies only pay fines where they are legally insurable and not excluded. Check the regulatory fines wording on your policy.

Does cyber insurance cover ransomware?

Many cyber policies include cover for cyber extortion, which can include the costs of responding to a ransomware demand, restoring systems and lost income while systems are down. Insurers often require you to report the incident and get their consent before acting, so check the conditions before you need them.

Is invoice fraud covered by cyber insurance?

Not always. Payment diversion and invoice fraud, where staff are tricked into paying a fraudster, may be excluded, sub-limited or offered as a separate social engineering extension. Ask specifically how your policy treats it, because it is one of the most common losses.

What must a business do after a data breach under POPIA?

Under section 22 of POPIA, a responsible party must notify the Information Regulator and the affected people as soon as reasonably possible after discovering that personal information has been accessed or acquired by an unauthorised person. Notify your insurer at the same time, as cyber policies commonly cover the cost of the notification process.

Will cyber insurance pay if we didn’t have basic security in place?

It depends on what you told the insurer and what the policy requires. If the proposal form said controls such as multi-factor authentication or backups were in place and they weren’t, the insurer may dispute the claim. Answer the security questions accurately and keep the controls running.
