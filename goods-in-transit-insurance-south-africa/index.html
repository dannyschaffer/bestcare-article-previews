Goods in transit insurance in South Africa: what it covers, what drives the price, and when you need marine cargo instead

If your business moves stock, materials or equipment by road, the goods are exposed from the moment they’re loaded until they’re signed for. Here’s what transit cover is for, what insurers look at when they price it, the conditions that most often decide a claim, and where marine cargo cover takes over.

Quick answer Goods in transit insurance covers loss of or damage to goods while they’re being moved, and usually while they’re loaded and unloaded, up to the limit on your schedule. It’s typically arranged for road and rail movements within South Africa, in your own vehicles, with hired carriers, or both. The price depends mainly on what you carry, the most you carry in one load, your routes and your security. Imports and exports with a sea or air leg are usually insured under marine cargo cover instead, and the sale terms decide who must buy it.

What goods in transit insurance is for

Transit cover is about the goods, not the vehicle. Your motor policy deals with damage to the bakkie or truck. Goods in transit cover deals with the load: stock on its way to a customer, materials going to site, equipment moving between branches. South Africa’s Insurance Act describes the transport class of insurance as covering “damage or loss resulting from the conveyance, storage, treatment and handling of goods in transit, irrespective of the form of transport” (Insurance Act 18 of 2017, Table 2).

In practice, a goods in transit section commonly responds to events such as:

  • an accident, overturning or fire involving the carrying vehicle
  • theft or hijacking of the vehicle with the goods on it, often subject to security conditions
  • damage while the goods are loaded or unloaded, where that’s included on the schedule
  • in some policies, the costs of clearing debris or recovering goods after an accident

What’s included varies between policies. The schedule and wording decide, so treat this list as the questions to check, not as a description of any policy.

Own vehicles, hired carriers and couriers

The first thing an insurer will ask is who’s doing the carrying. Policies often treat these differently, and some only include one.

Your own vehicles

Goods carried in vehicles the business owns or operates. Insurers usually want to know the vehicles, who drives them, and how they’re secured when parked. If the vehicle is a bakkie that also does private driving, check its own motor cover is rated for business use. Our bakkie business-use guide explains why.

Hired carriers

Goods moved by a transport company you contract. The carrier may carry its own goods in transit cover, but its liability to you is usually set by its contract terms and may be capped well below the value of your load. Your own cover can fill that gap.

Couriers and postal services

Courier terms commonly limit what they’ll pay for loss or damage, sometimes to a small amount per parcel unless you declare a value and pay for extra protection. Don’t assume the courier’s insurance protects your interest in the goods.

What drives the price of goods in transit insurance

There’s no standard rate. Insurers price the load, the route and the security, so the same business can get very different quotes depending on what it tells them. These are the details insurers commonly ask for:

Type of goodsElectronics, cigarettes, alcohol, metals and fast-moving consumer goods are attractive to thieves. Fragile or perishable goods are more easily damaged.

Security weighs heavily for road freight in Gauteng. Police reported in June 2026 that 25 of South Africa’s top 30 truck hijacking hotspots are in Gauteng, with increases in Alberton, Orange Farm, Germiston, Mamelodi East and Kempton Park in the first quarter of the year (EWN, 10 June 2026).

Compare more than the premium: ask for the excess on theft and hijacking claims, the limit per vehicle, and any security conditions in the same breath. A cheaper premium with a high theft excess, or a condition you can’t meet on your routes, can cost more when you claim.

Conditions that commonly decide a transit claim

Most disputes on transit claims aren’t about whether the goods were lost. They’re about whether a condition was met. Look for these on your schedule and wording:

  • Unattended vehicles. Many policies restrict theft cover when a loaded vehicle is left unattended, or require it to be locked with keys removed and alarms set.
  • Overnight loads. Goods left in a vehicle overnight may only be covered in a locked building or a secured yard.
  • Tracking. Where tracking is a condition, check what happens if the unit isn’t working at the time of loss.
  • Packing. Damage caused by inadequate packing is commonly excluded.
  • Delay and loss of market. Losses because goods arrived late are commonly excluded unless a specific extension applies.
  • Refrigerated goods. Spoilage after a refrigeration unit breaks down is often only covered by a separate extension.
  • Limits per vehicle. If a load is worth more than the per-vehicle limit, the claim may be capped at the limit.

None of these are universal, and some can be changed by agreement. They’re the questions worth settling before you need to claim.

Goods in transit or marine cargo: which one applies?

Goods in transit cover is usually arranged for movements by road or rail within South Africa, and sometimes into neighbouring countries. Marine cargo cover is usually used for imports and exports, especially where goods travel by sea or air for part of the journey. Despite the name, marine cargo policies often run “warehouse to warehouse”, including the road legs at each end.

Goods in transit and marine cargo compared (typical arrangements; policies vary)
Goods in transitMarine cargo
Typical journeysLocal and long-distance road or rail within South Africa, sometimes neighbouring countriesImports and exports by sea, air, road or a combination
Common basisInsurer’s own wording, often a section of a business policyOften the Institute Cargo Clauses (A), (B) or (C)
How it’s arrangedAnnual cover with a limit per vehicle or loadPer shipment, or an open policy declaring shipments as they happen
Who buys itUsually the business that owns or carries the goodsDepends on the sale terms (Incoterms)

The Institute Cargo Clauses come in three levels. Clauses (A) are broad, “all risks” cover; Clauses (C) cover a narrower list of named events and are described by the ICC as “sometimes referred to as minimum cover” (ICC Academy).

For an import or export, the contract’s Incoterms rule decides who must insure. Under Incoterms 2020, a seller selling CIF must buy insurance for the buyer to at least Clauses (C), and CIF is only for sea and inland waterway transport. A seller selling CIP must insure to Clauses (A) (ICC Academy). If you’re the buyer on CIF terms, the seller’s minimum cover may be narrower than you expect for finished goods, so it’s worth checking whether to top it up. Under other rules, check in the contract who carries the risk at each stage before assuming anyone has insured the goods.

Key takeaways

  • Goods in transit cover is for the load, not the vehicle, and usually includes loading and unloading where scheduled.
  • Policies treat your own vehicles, hired carriers and couriers differently. A carrier’s or courier’s liability is often capped.
  • Price depends on the goods, the maximum load value, routes, security and claims history. Compare excesses and conditions, not just premiums.
  • Unattended-vehicle, overnight, tracking and packing conditions decide many claims.
  • Imports and exports usually need marine cargo cover, and the Incoterms rule decides who buys it.

Goods in transit insurance FAQs

General answers about goods in transit and marine cargo cover in South Africa. Your schedule and policy wording decide what applies to you.

What is goods in transit insurance?

It is cover for loss of or damage to goods while they are being moved, and usually while they are loaded and unloaded, subject to the limits and conditions on your schedule. South Africa’s Insurance Act describes the transport class as covering loss from the conveyance, storage, treatment and handling of goods in transit, whatever the form of transport.

How much does goods in transit insurance cost?

There is no standard price. Insurers usually look at what you carry, the most you carry in one load, how much you move in a year, your routes, your vehicles or carriers, security such as tracking and parking, and your claims history. Ask for the premium and the excess together, because a low premium with a high theft excess can cost more at claim time.

Does goods in transit insurance cover theft and hijacking?

Many policies include theft and hijacking, but often with conditions, such as the vehicle being locked, tracked or not left unattended, or goods not being left in the vehicle overnight. Read those conditions closely, because they are a common reason claims are disputed.

Is goods in transit the same as marine cargo insurance?

No. Goods in transit is usually arranged for movements by road or rail within South Africa, and sometimes neighbouring countries. Marine cargo is usually used for imports and exports that include a sea or air leg, and is often written on the Institute Cargo Clauses.

Does the courier’s insurance cover my goods?

Not automatically. A courier’s or carrier’s liability is usually limited by its terms and conditions, and may be far lower than the value of what you sent. Check those terms and decide whether your own transit cover is needed.

Does goods in transit insurance cover goods in my own bakkie?

It can. Many policies distinguish between goods carried in your own vehicles and goods sent with hired carriers, and some only include one of the two. Tell the insurer which applies, and check that your vehicle’s own policy is rated for business use.

Who insures the goods on an import or export?

It depends on the sale terms. Under the Incoterms 2020 rules CIF and CIP, the seller must buy insurance for the buyer: at least the minimum Institute Cargo Clauses (C) for CIF and the broader Clauses (A) for CIP. Under other terms, check in the contract who carries the risk at each stage.
