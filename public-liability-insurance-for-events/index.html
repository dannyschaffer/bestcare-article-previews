Do you need public liability cover for a one-off event?

A year-end market, a school fete, a fun run, a wedding at a farm venue: one-off events bring crowds, temporary structures, food and hired equipment together for a day. If someone gets hurt, the organiser is the first person they look to. Here’s when South African law, municipalities and venues expect public liability insurance for events, and how to work out what you need.

Quick answer Often, yes. For events covered by the Safety at Sports and Recreational Events Act, the organiser, controlling body or venue owner “must ensure that public liability insurance, as prescribed, is in place for an event” (SASREA, Act 2 of 2010, s 25). City event processes ask for it too: Johannesburg’s Disaster Management unit requires “confirmation of public liability insurance” (City of Johannesburg), and Cape Town’s Events By-law requires it where an activity “may put the public at risk” (City of Cape Town Events By-law). Even when nobody asks, the organiser can be held liable if a guest is injured through negligence.

When public liability insurance for events is required

Public liability insurance for events can be required by law, by a municipal permit or by the venue’s booking terms, and many events trigger more than one. The widest rule is SASREA. It defines an event as “sporting, entertainment, recreational, religious, cultural, exhibitional, organisational or similar activities hosted at a stadium, venue or along a route”, and section 25 requires public liability insurance to be in place, “sourced from a person registered or authorised” as a short-term insurer (SASREA). The Act doesn’t apply to gatherings under the Regulation of Gatherings Act, such as protests.

Municipalities then add their own process. In Johannesburg, event organisers work through a Joint Operations Committee, where Disaster Management issues “a final approval for any temporary structure and confirmation of public liability insurance”, and the metro police require “an indemnity form” signed by the organiser and an application 30 days before the event (City of Johannesburg JOC stakeholders). Cape Town’s application checklist lists “Confirmation of Public Liability insurance” among the documents that “MUST accompany the initial Event Application” (City of Cape Town event guidelines).

Small private events are treated more lightly. Cape Town’s by-law doesn’t require a permit for events of fewer than 200 people with no amplified sound and no temporary structures, or for “small events such as family and community events held on private property or purpose built venues” (City of Cape Town Events By-law). Rules differ by municipality, so check your own. Not needing a permit doesn’t remove the risk of being sued, though; for a party at home, the personal liability section of a home policy is the usual place to look.

Why organisers can be liable when someone gets hurt

An organiser can be held liable if a reasonable person in their position would have foreseen the risk of harm and taken reasonable steps to prevent it, and they didn’t. That’s the test for negligence South African courts have applied since Kruger v Coetzee 1966 (2) SA 428 (A). At an event, that might mean a loose cable across a walkway, a stage built without a sign-off, a jumping castle with no anchor, or too few marshals on a busy route.

The claimant still has to prove negligence and that it caused the harm. In the Salie case, a spectator knocked over by an elite runner at a Cape Town road race claimed R718 000 from both the organiser, Western Province Athletics, and the runner, arguing among other things that there weren’t enough marshals. The claim against the organiser failed at trial (Salie v Western Province Athletics [2021] ZAWCHC 52), and a full bench agreed on appeal. The runner, though, was held liable for 30% of the damages for not keeping a proper lookout, and the Supreme Court of Appeal confirmed that in 2025 (Kalmer v Davids NO [2025] ZASCA 26). Participants can carry liability too, not only the organiser. Winning in court still costs money, and public liability cover usually pays defence costs as well as damages. That makes it worth asking how far a disclaimer really protects you.

Do “enter at your own risk” signs protect organisers?

Not on their own. Since the Consumer Protection Act, a notice that limits a supplier’s liability or makes the consumer accept a risk must be in plain language and drawn to the consumer’s attention conspicuously. For risks that could cause “serious injury or death”, the supplier “must specifically draw the fact, nature and potential effect of that risk to the attention of the consumer”, and the consumer must have assented to it, for example by signing (Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008, s 49). A term that tries to exempt a supplier from liability for its own gross negligence is prohibited outright under section 51.

The courts read indemnities narrowly too. Before the CPA, the Supreme Court of Appeal upheld a clear exclusion notice at an amusement park in Durban’s Water Wonderland v Botha (1999). But in Duffield v Lilyfontein School [2011] ZAECGHC 3, where an adventure race entrant fell from a zip wire, the court held that an indemnity form promising “stringent safety measures” protected the organisers only if they had actually put those measures in place (SAFLII). Signs and forms help, but they don’t replace safety measures or insurance. Neither does relying on your suppliers’ cover.

Suppliers, venues and who carries what

At most events, several businesses share the risk: the venue, caterers, the jumping castle hirer, the sound and stage company, and security. The CPA can make each of them liable for harm caused by goods. A supplier of services who “applies, supplies, installs or provides access to any goods” is treated as a supplier of those goods, and where more than one person is liable, “their liability is joint and several” (Consumer Protection Act, s 61). An injured guest can claim from any of them, and the organiser may be first in line.

Ask every supplier for proof of their own public liability cover, and keep copies with your event file. How much public liability insurance is enough for an event?

There’s no single national minimum; the right limit depends on the size of the crowd, the activities and what your permit or venue contract asks for. Johannesburg’s events checklist simply has a blank for the public liability “Amount: R” (City of Johannesburg events checklist), rather than a fixed figure. Venues, municipalities and tenders often set their own minimums, so start with the highest figure anyone involved is asking for, then consider the worst realistic injury. The Ellis Park inquiry into the 2001 stadium disaster, in which 43 people died, is a reminder of how badly crowd safety can go wrong when responsibilities are unclear (Ellis Park Commission of Inquiry report).

For a one-off event, cover is usually arranged for the event dates only, including set-up and breakdown days. Businesses that run events regularly may be better served by an annual policy. Key takeaways

  • SASREA section 25 requires public liability insurance for events it covers, which include sporting, entertainment, religious, cultural and similar events at venues or along routes.
  • Johannesburg and Cape Town both ask for confirmation of public liability insurance in their event processes.
  • Small private events may not need a permit, but the organiser can still be sued for negligence.
  • “Enter at your own risk” signs must meet CPA rules, and can’t exclude liability for gross negligence.
  • Under the CPA, suppliers of goods can be jointly liable, so get every supplier’s certificate of cover.
  • Set the limit from what the permit, venue or tender requires, and the worst realistic claim.

Event public liability FAQs

General answers about public liability cover for events in South Africa.

Do I need public liability insurance for an event?

Often, yes. SASREA section 25 requires the organiser, controlling body or venue owner to ensure public liability insurance is in place for events the Act covers, and municipal permit processes in cities such as Johannesburg and Cape Town ask for confirmation of cover. Venues often require it in their booking terms too.

Does public liability insurance cover events?

An annual business public liability policy may or may not extend to events you organise, depending on its wording and the business activities on the schedule. For a one-off event, organisers often arrange cover for the event dates, including set-up and breakdown. Check the wording before you rely on an existing policy.

Can I get public liability insurance for a single event?

Yes, cover can be arranged for one event and its dates, subject to the insurer’s underwriting. The insurer will usually ask about the type of event, expected attendance, venue, activities, alcohol and suppliers.

How much is public liability insurance for an event?

The cost depends on the limit, the number of people, the activities, the venue, whether alcohol is served and the event’s length. A quote is the only reliable figure. The limit itself is often set by the permit, the venue or a tender.

Who is responsible for public liability insurance at an event?

Under SASREA, the controlling body, event organiser or stadium or venue owner must ensure cover is in place. In practice the organiser usually arranges it, and suppliers such as caterers, stage companies and jumping castle hirers should carry their own.

Does an “enter at your own risk” sign protect the organiser?

Not on its own. Under the Consumer Protection Act, risk notices must be in plain language, conspicuous and, for risks of serious injury or death, specifically drawn to the person’s attention and accepted. A term can’t exempt a supplier from liability for its own gross negligence.

Do I need public liability insurance for a private party at home?

A private family party usually doesn’t need an event permit, and Cape Town’s by-law exempts small family events on private property. You can still be liable if a guest is injured through negligence, so check whether your home policy includes personal liability cover.

Do stallholders at a market need their own public liability insurance?

It depends on the market organiser’s terms. Some require each stallholder to carry their own cover, others include stallholders in the market’s policy. Ask the organiser in writing before the market.
