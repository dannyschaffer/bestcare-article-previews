What public liability insurance covers in South Africa, and who needs it

Public liability insurance means cover for the claims other people make against your business when they are hurt, or their property is damaged, because of what you do.

What public liability insurance means for a South African business

Public liability insurance covers your legal liability to third parties for accidental bodily injury or property damage connected to your business activities or premises. “Third parties” means people outside the business, such as customers, visitors, passers-by, clients and their property. It doesn’t mean you, your employees or the things you own.

The key word is liability. The policy doesn’t pay simply because someone was hurt. It responds when the business is legally responsible, usually because of negligence under South African delict law, or where a statute imposes liability. It then pays the compensation the business owes and, in most wordings, the cost of defending the claim. How defence costs sit against the limit varies between policies, so it’s worth checking. That legal-liability trigger explains both what the cover does and where its edges are, so it’s worth being specific about each side.

What public liability typically covers, and what it leaves out

Most public liability policies cover third-party injury, third-party property damage and related legal costs. They exclude risks that belong under other types of cover. The exact list is always set by the policy wording and schedule, but the pattern below is common across South African business policies.

A 2026 ruling every business owner should know

In January 2026, the Supreme Court of Appeal refused to overturn a finding that a retailer was liable for a shopper’s slip-and-fall, even though it had outsourced floor cleaning to a contractor. The case, Pick ’n Pay Retailers v Williams [2026] ZASCA 07, arose from a fall on 13 November 2017 at a store in N1 City Mall, Goodwood. The shopper slipped on an oily substance in an aisle and injured her hip and shoulder. She claimed past and future medical expenses, loss of earnings and general damages.

The Western Cape High Court found the retailer liable in 2023. The Supreme Court of Appeal dismissed the retailer’s application for reconsideration on 26 January 2026, more than eight years after the fall. The court relied on the long-standing test that the person in control of premises must

“take reasonable steps to keep his premises reasonably safe at all times when members of the public may be using them.”Quoted by the Supreme Court of Appeal in Pick ’n Pay Retailers v Williams (2026), from Probst and Cenprop v Holtzhauzen

Three lessons carry over to any business that deals with the public:

  • Outsourcing the task doesn’t outsource the liability. The retailer had a cleaning contract, but it still had to show that reasonable steps were taken to keep the floor safe.
  • Claims take years. Legal costs build up long before any compensation is paid, which is why defence-cost cover matters.
  • Contracts help, but don’t replace insurance. The high court declared the cleaning contractor liable to indemnify the retailer under their agreement. That is a separate fight, and it depends on the contractor being able to pay.

Premises are only one source of exposure. The goods a business sells can create liability too, and here South African law is stricter still.

Selling goods adds strict liability under the Consumer Protection Act

Section 61 of the Consumer Protection Act 68 of 2008 makes producers, importers, distributors and retailers liable for harm caused by unsafe or defective goods, whether or not they were negligent. A consumer doesn’t have to prove fault, and the parties in the supply chain can be held jointly and severally liable (Cliffe Dekker Hofmeyr, Product liability overview). Harm under the Act includes death, injury, illness, property damage and certain economic loss.

The Act does give defences. For example, a retailer may not be liable where it would be unreasonable to expect it to have discovered the defect, or where the defect didn’t exist when the goods were supplied. Claims must generally be brought within three years. For a shop, café, online seller or importer, it’s worth checking whether products liability is included or needs to be added. Who needs public liability insurance?

Any business whose premises, people or work could injure someone else or damage their property should consider public liability cover. For many, a lease or contract makes the decision for them. There is no general law making it compulsory, but landlords, shopping centres, event venues, municipalities, main contractors and corporate clients routinely ask for proof of cover at a stated limit.

Once you know you need it, the next question is how much cover to buy.

How much public liability cover do you need?

Choose your limit of indemnity based on the worst realistic claim your business could face, and on any minimum your contracts require. Don’t just pick the cheapest option. Limits in the South African market commonly range from R1 million to R10 million. Some providers offer fixed options such as R1 million, R2.5 million, R5 million, R7.5 million and R10 million (Bi-me).

  • Per event or aggregate: check whether the limit applies to each claim, to all claims in the policy year, or both.
  • Defence costs: check whether legal costs sit inside the limit, which reduces what’s left for compensation, or are paid in addition to it.
  • Sub-limits: extensions such as products liability or spread of fire can have lower limits than the headline figure.
  • Excess: the amount you pay on each claim, which may be higher for certain events.
  • Contract terms: leases and contracts may ask for a minimum limit, for you to name them as an additional insured, or for a “hold harmless” clause the policy may not fully cover.

Premiums depend on your activity, turnover, footfall, where you work, your claims history and the limit you choose. For context, MiWay reports that many small and medium-sized businesses pay between R200 and R5,500 a month for business insurance overall, not only for liability cover (MiWay, 2026). The only reliable figure is a quote based on your actual risk.

Public liability insurance FAQs

These answers describe how public liability commonly works in South Africa. Your policy wording and schedule decide what is actually covered.

What does public liability insurance mean in simple terms?

Public liability insurance protects a business when it is held legally liable for accidental bodily injury to, or damage to the property of, third parties such as customers, visitors or members of the public. It typically pays the compensation awarded and the legal costs of defending the claim, up to the policy’s limit of indemnity.

Is public liability insurance compulsory in South Africa?

There is no general law requiring every business to carry it. In practice, many landlords, shopping centres, event venues, clients, municipalities and main contractors require proof of public liability cover, often at a specified limit, before they will sign a lease or contract.

Does public liability insurance cover my employees if they get hurt at work?

Generally not. Injuries to employees are usually dealt with under the Compensation for Occupational Injuries and Diseases Act (COIDA), with employers’ liability insurance available for certain claims beyond that. Public liability focuses on third parties.

Does public liability cover bad advice or professional mistakes?

Usually not. Financial loss caused by advice, designs or professional services is normally a professional indemnity question. Public liability responds to accidental injury or physical damage to third-party property.

What is the difference between public liability and products liability?

Public liability covers injury or damage arising from your business activities and premises. Products liability covers injury or damage caused by goods you manufacture, import, distribute or sell. Under section 61 of the Consumer Protection Act, suppliers can be liable for harm from unsafe or defective goods without the consumer proving negligence.

How much public liability cover does a small business need?

Common limits in South Africa range from R1 million to R10 million or more. The right figure depends on how many people you interact with, where you work, what could go wrong and what your contracts or lease require. A broker can help you match the limit to the actual exposure.

Can I rely on my cleaning or security contractor’s insurance instead?

Not safely. In Pick ’n Pay v Williams (2026), the Supreme Court of Appeal left the retailer liable to an injured shopper even though it had outsourced floor cleaning. A contractor’s indemnity may help you recover costs, but your own cover is what responds when the claim is made against you.

What information will an insurer want for a public liability quote?

Expect questions about your business activities, premises and locations, turnover, number of visitors or customers, work done at clients’ sites, contracts and required limits, previous claims or incidents, and your risk controls, such as cleaning schedules and safety procedures.
