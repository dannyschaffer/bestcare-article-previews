Contractors all risk insurance: who carries the risk on site

Every building contract decides who carries the risk if the half-finished works burn, flood or get stripped overnight, and who has to insure it. Here’s what contractors all risk insurance in South Africa covers, how the JBCC and GCC contracts split the responsibility, how to set the sum insured, and the gaps that surprise contractors and clients at claim time.

Quick answer Contractors all risk (CAR) insurance, also called contract works insurance, may pay for physical loss of or damage to the works while they’re being built, including materials on site and temporary works, and it usually sits alongside public liability cover for injury or damage to other people. Who must take it out depends on the contract. The JBCC Principal Building Agreement lets the Contract Data place it with the employer, the contractor or both, while the CIDB’s guide to the General Conditions of Contract says “unless it is stated in the Contract Data that the Employer will take out the insurance, the Contractor must take out the insurance” (CIDB guide to the GCC). Repairing defective work itself is generally not covered.

What contractors all risk insurance covers

Contractors all risk insurance protects the project itself: the permanent works, the materials delivered to site and the temporary works needed to build them. The JBCC describes the main section as “Contract Works Insurance (CWI) (including materials and goods, temporary works)”, insured for the contract sum (JBCC guide to completion and payment (2014)). Around that core, a construction insurance programme usually adds several related covers.

The CIDB lists the insurances a contractor can arrange on engineering and construction contracts as “Contract Works Insurance, Public Liability Insurances, SASRIA Special Risks Insurance, Removal of Support Insurance, general insurance cover for plant owned or hired by them, and Professional Indemnity Insurance” (CIDB, Managing Construction Procurement Risks). Some of these are sections of one CAR policy and some are separate policies, depending on the insurer.

Who takes out CAR cover: JBCC vs GCC

The building contract decides who insures the works, so read the Contract Data before you buy anything. South Africa’s two most common standard forms start from different defaults.

JBCC Principal Building Agreement

The JBCC Contract Data has two insurance schedules, “Insurances by employer” and “Insurances by contractor”, each listing contract works insurance [10.1.1], supplementary insurance [10.1.2], public liability insurance [10.1.3], removal of lateral support insurance [10.1.4] and other insurances [10.1.5] (JBCC PBA Edition 6.2 Contract Data). The parties tick yes or no against each line. Where works reach practical completion in sections, the JBCC’s guidance is that “the contract works insurance must be effected by the employer to prevent conflict that may arise from multiple practical completions possibly insured by different insurers” (JBCC Guide B).

CIDB General Conditions of Contract

On civil engineering contracts using the GCC, the contractor insures unless the Contract Data says the employer will. The CIDB’s guide adds a point many contractors miss: “It does not matter who takes out insurance, the Contractor is still responsible for any loss or damage to the site and the materials on site” (CIDB guide to the GCC). If the insurance falls short, the contractor carries the gap. That guide was written for GCC 2004, so check the clause numbers in the edition your contract uses.

Subcontractors and public sector work

The CIDB notes that “most standard forms of engineering and construction contracts require the contractor to effect a range of insurances and to extend such cover to subcontractors” (CIDB). Public clients often amend the standard terms. A Department of Public Works and Infrastructure tender, for example, says the contractor “shall bear the full risk of damage to and/or destruction of the works by whatever cause during construction” and must insure the works, public liability and, where applicable, removal of lateral support “in favour of the employer as beneficiary” (DPWI tender CPT1001-24). Once you know who insures, the next question is for how much.

How much contractors all risk insurance do I need?

The contract works sum insured normally starts with the full contract value, then adds what the contract value leaves out. The JBCC Contract Data builds it up from the contract sum, works by direct contractors, “free issue” materials supplied by the employer, and “escalation, professional fees and reinstatement costs if not included above” (JBCC PBA 6.2). On alterations and additions, it asks for the “reinstatement value of existing structures with or including new works”, because a fire on a renovation can destroy the old building as well as the new work.

  • Contract sum: the value of the permanent and temporary works, including tax where the schedule asks for it.
  • Escalation: price increases over a long build, so the cover keeps pace with the cost of rebuilding later in the contract.
  • Professional fees and demolition: the cost of redesign, clearing debris and starting again after a major loss.
  • Free issue and direct contractors: materials and work the employer supplies outside your contract.
  • Existing structures: on renovation work, the reinstatement value of what was already there.

Getting this wrong has the same effect as on any property policy: if the works are underinsured, the average clause can reduce every claim in proportion, as we explain in our guide to underinsurance and the average clause and our explainer on what sum insured means. The amount also has to last for the right length of time.

When cover starts and stops

Contract works cover typically runs from possession of the site to practical completion, then reduces while the contractor finishes snags and fixes defects. The JBCC lists what follows practical completion: possession of the site goes back to the employer, the “contractor’s all risk insurance reduces until final completion”, the “employer’s building insurance commences” and “public liability insurance remains in force until final completion” (JBCC Advisory Note 19).

This handover is where gaps appear. If the employer moves in before arranging building insurance, or the contractor’s policy ends at practical completion while snag work continues, damage in that window can fall between two policies. Check both dates before the certificate is signed. The other common gap is what the policy excludes.

What CAR insurance usually won’t pay for

CAR insurance pays for accidental damage to the works, not for fixing work that was wrong to begin with. The JBCC puts it plainly: a construction all risk policy “generally does not provide coverage for claims by discontented owners for the cost to repair or replace allegedly defective work” (JBCC Advisory Note 21).

The distinction that matters is between the defect and the damage it causes. In Allianz Insurance Ltd v RHI Refractories Africa [2007] ZASCA 174, the policy excluded the cost to “replace, repair or rectify any defect”, but limited that exclusion where “unintended damage” resulted from the defect. When an epoxy lining in an acid plant failed and caused damage, the Supreme Court of Appeal held that the resulting damage could be claimed, even though it was foreseeable (SAFLII). In Zurich Insurance Company South Africa v Gauteng Provincial Government [2022] ZASCA 127, about water ingress in the Gautrain tunnels, the court held that the insured tunnel works included the surrounding rock mass essential to the tunnel, so the province’s claim succeeded (SAFLII). Both cases turned on the exact wording, which is why the wording matters more than the policy name.

Other risks are allocated elsewhere by the contract. The CIDB’s risk table shows the employer transferring the risk of faulty design to the professionals and requiring them to insure, and late completion staying with the employer, who “may require some penalty from the Contractor” (CIDB). Penalties for delay are a contract matter, not a works damage claim. Commonly excluded or limited items include:

  • the cost of putting right defective design, materials or workmanship;
  • wear and tear, and gradual deterioration;
  • penalties, delay damages and other financial loss that isn’t physical damage;
  • theft without signs of forced entry, or from an unsecured site, where the policy has security conditions;
  • riot and public disorder damage, unless Sasria cover is in place.

Exact exclusions differ by insurer and policy, so ask for the wording, not just the schedule. The risks on South African sites show why those details are worth checking.

The risks South African sites face

Theft, disruption, weather and accidents all put works under construction at risk, and some of the numbers are large. Deputy Minister of Public Works Sihle Zikalala has said disruptions by so-called construction mafia groups cost South Africa an estimated R63 billion between 2019 and 2024 (CBN (July 2025)). SAFCEC, citing National Treasury, reported over 180 hijacked projects, and 745 extortion cases with 240 arrests since November 2024 (SAFCEC newsletter (July 2025)). In the Western Cape, the Master Builders Association reported that “at least six substantial projects valued at more than R400-million were halted” in 2024 because of syndicate activity (Sunday World (January 2025)).

People are at risk too. The Department of Employment and Labour says “at least 1.5 to 2 fatalities occur on a weekly basis” in construction, that compensation for construction injuries and diseases exceeded R500 million in a year, and that “more than 70 per cent of incidents dealt with could have been prevented” (Department of Employment and Labour (October 2024)). The 2014 Construction Regulations make clients responsible for health and safety from design and tender onwards.

Weather adds to this, from Highveld hail to Western Cape floods; our hail and storm claims guide covers what to record after a storm. Whether a loss linked to disruption or extortion on site is covered depends on the wording and on whether Sasria cover is in place, so raise it before work starts. Making a claim on the works

Report damage to the works as soon as possible, protect the site from further loss, and keep photographs, delivery notes, progress reports and the principal agent’s records. The same evidence habits apply as on any claim; our guide on how to make an insurance claim step by step sets them out. If a claim is rejected, our guide to rejected claims and the National Financial Ombud explains how to challenge the decision. Contractors who carry goods between yards and sites should also look at goods in transit insurance, and those whose income stops after a major loss at their own premises at business interruption cover.

Key takeaways

  • CAR (contract works) insurance is for physical loss or damage to the works, materials and temporary works during the contract.
  • The contract decides who insures: JBCC Contract Data can place cover with the employer, the contractor or both; under the GCC the contractor insures unless the Contract Data says otherwise.
  • Under the GCC, the contractor stays responsible for loss or damage on site even when the employer insures.
  • Set the sum insured at the contract value plus escalation, professional fees, free-issue materials and, on renovations, existing structures.
  • Contract works cover reduces at practical completion while public liability continues to final completion, so plan the handover.
  • Repairing defective work itself is generally not covered, but resulting damage can be, depending on the wording.

Contractors all risk insurance FAQs

General answers about contractors all risk insurance in South Africa.

What does contractors all risk insurance cover?

Contractors all risk insurance may cover physical loss of or damage to the works under construction, materials on site and temporary works, usually alongside public liability cover for injury or damage to third parties. Related covers such as removal of lateral support, plant and Sasria special risks may be sections of the same policy or separate policies.

What does contractors all risk insurance mean?

It means insurance for the risks to a construction project while it is being built, on an all risks basis: the policy covers accidental physical loss or damage from any cause that isn’t excluded. It is also called contract works insurance or construction all risks insurance.

Who is responsible for contractors all risk insurance, the employer or the contractor?

The building contract decides. JBCC Contract Data has separate schedules for insurances by the employer and by the contractor. Under the GCC, the CIDB’s guide says the contractor takes out the insurance unless the Contract Data says the employer will, and the contractor stays responsible for loss or damage on site either way.

How much contractors all risk insurance do I need?

Start with the full contract value, then add escalation, professional fees and demolition costs, materials supplied by the employer, work by direct contractors and, on alterations, the reinstatement value of the existing structure. Underinsuring can trigger the average clause and reduce claims.

Does contractors all risk insurance cover defective workmanship?

Generally not the cost of fixing the defect itself. The JBCC notes that a construction all risk policy generally doesn’t cover owners’ claims for repairing or replacing defective work. Damage that a defect causes to other parts of the works may be claimable, depending on the wording, as the Supreme Court of Appeal found in Allianz v RHI Refractories.

When does contractors all risk cover end?

Usually at practical completion, with reduced cover until final completion while defects are fixed. Under the JBCC, the employer’s building insurance starts at practical completion and public liability stays in force until final completion. Check the dates in your contract and policy schedule.

Does contractors all risk insurance cover injuries to my workers?

Not usually. Injuries to employees fall under COIDA, with employers’ liability insurance for claims beyond it. The public liability section of a construction policy is for injury or damage to third parties, such as neighbours and members of the public.

Is contractors all risk insurance required for government tenders?

Many public tenders require it. A Department of Public Works and Infrastructure tender, for example, requires the contractor to insure the works, public liability and, where applicable, removal of lateral support in favour of the employer. Read the insurance clauses in each tender document.
